# Chapter 2 — Coda

## Return to Coda

Coda announced itself before it was visible.

At sixty atmospheres the colony's docking beacons began their low-frequency pulse — a navigational signal transmitted through the atmospheric medium rather than through space, felt rather than seen, a rhythmic pressure against the hull that Sable had learned in her first week aboard the *Meridian* to distinguish from weather and from the ship's own systems. It came in threes: two short pulses and one long, repeating every forty seconds. She felt it now as a faint but recognizable pattern across her lower back as she stood at the viewport, watching the bruised violet of the upper atmosphere give way to the amber glow of Coda's outer illumination ring.

The colony occupied a stable pressure band at approximately thirty-one standard atmospheres — high enough above the crushing depths to allow structural integrity without constant reinforcement cycling, low enough to use the gas giant's atmosphere as both thermal insulation and radiation shielding. From the outside, approaching from below, it looked like something that had no right to exist: a horizontal structure nearly two kilometers across, its underside a maze of docking cradles and equipment gantries and the enormous pressure-differential membranes that kept the inhabited sections sealed against the atmosphere. The outer ring was industrial — storage, processing, the acoustic fossil refinement labs where raw crystalline wavefronts were prepared for Archive cataloguing and commercial sale. The inner ring was residential, administrative, the Archive's primary facility.

Sable had docked at Coda forty-seven times. She had counted, once, in her second season, when she had needed something precise to think about during a difficult ascent.

She knew the approach by its vibration signature. The docking cradle locks fired in sequence — three impacts, each transmitted through the hull as a distinct percussive thud, each slightly different in timbre because each cradle was slightly different in age and wear. The magnetic grapples that secured the ship's dorsal hull to the cradle framework engaged with a sustained pressure against the top of the ship, felt at her feet as a low, even compression, the vibration of the electromagnets cycling at their holding frequency.

Then the pressurization bridge connected, and the vibration changed entirely.

A city's worth of mechanical systems communicated themselves through the structure of Coda's dock. Not cleanly — it was nothing like the *Meridian's* known harmonics, nothing she had mapped or named — but in the aggregate it was familiar. Forty-seven dockings had given her a composite sense of what Coda felt like, the way a long-time resident of a house develops an unconscious awareness of its particular creaks. She knew when the atmospheric processors in sector four were running a maintenance cycle. She knew the particular shudder of the freight elevator along the colony's spine. She knew the difference between a normal docking floor and a docking floor with a crowd on it.

The dock felt normal. She had half-expected it not to.

Thresh came through the forward hatch with the post-dive documentation on a tablet, already dressed out of his dive suit, moving with the slightly stiff posture of someone who had been in an uncomfortable position for too long. He handed her the tablet without preamble. She read it.

The standard post-dive fields were complete: dive depth, trawl duration, atmospheric conditions, equipment status, capture pod inventory. Two entries under *Captures:* one supernova fossil, class two, crystal density 0.87, four-point-two billion years estimated age, premium classification, Archive credit valuation pending specialist review. One anomalous capture, classification pending, crystal density — and here the field was blank, replaced with a flag she had not seen before in three seasons of post-dive documentation.

The flag read: *CLASSIFICATION SYSTEM UNABLE TO PROCESS. MANUAL REVIEW REQUIRED.*

She looked at the flag for a moment. Then she looked at Thresh.

His hands: *Archive sent a receipt acknowledgment for the anomaly thirty minutes ago. Standard intake protocol. They want us in processing as soon as the ship is secured.*

She handed the tablet back. *Did they say why the classification failed?*

*No.*

She looked at the capture pod housing. The status light was still green. Had been green since 11:31. Whatever was in there — whatever shape had pressed through her nervous system and burned geometric fire along her nerve pathways — was now a bureaucratic problem for the Pan-Systemic Archive's intake department.

She picked up her jacket from the equipment rack. She put it on. She followed Thresh to the docking bridge.

## The Bridge

The bridge connecting the *Meridian* to Coda's dock was enclosed and pressurized — a standard telescoping umbilical, approximately fifteen meters long, lit by overhead fluorescents that buzzed at a frequency she felt as a faint, fast vibration in the bridge's deck plating. She had walked this bridge forty-seven times. The fluorescent buzz was always there. She had not consciously noticed it until now, because she was noticing everything now, cataloguing every sensation against the possibility that something was wrong with the catalogue itself.

The transducers were still running warm.

Not hot — not painful, not the burning geometry of the anomaly's signal. Just warm. A degree or two above their normal operating temperature, which should have been ambient, which should have been whatever temperature the surrounding tissue was. Passive hardware did not generate heat. She had noted it aboard the ship. She was noting it again now, with more precision, because the *Meridian* was behind her and she was walking through a structure she had walked through forty-seven times and she wanted to be certain that what she was feeling was the transducers and not the bridge.

It was the transducers. The bridge felt exactly as it always felt. The fluorescent buzz at her feet, the slight flex of the umbilical structure against the docking ring in the medium-pressure wind event currently running at approximately 140 kilometers per hour outside the colony's weather shields, the texture of the non-slip deck surface through her boot soles.

All known. All catalogued. All correct.

She walked through the docking bay's pressure door and into the colony proper, and the smell of it met her the way it always did — recycled atmosphere with a slight metallic edge, a trace of the chemical compounds used in the acoustic refinement labs on the outer ring, something organic underneath that she had always assumed was the collective biological presence of approximately four thousand people living in an enclosed space. She could not smell the anomaly. She could not smell the fossil. She did not know what she had expected.

The docking bay was busy with the end-of-shift changeover from the outer refinement labs. Workers in processing coveralls moved past her carrying equipment cases, pulling hand-trucks loaded with fossil transport containers, talking — she knew they were talking, could see the movement of mouths, the orientation of bodies toward each other — in the particular pattern of people who did this every day and had run out of things to say about it. Nobody looked at her. She was forty-seven dockings familiar.

Thresh touched her shoulder and pointed toward the Archive intake corridor.

She followed him into the corridor. Behind them, in the pressurized cargo transfer system, the capture pods from the *Meridian* were already moving.

## Archive Intake

The Pan-Systemic Archive's processing facility on Coda occupied most of the inner ring's lower level — a series of climate-controlled labs built around the central fossil vault, where the colony's entire accumulated catalogue of acoustic history was stored in a refrigerated crystalline array that Sable had been shown once, on orientation, and had not thought about much since. It was very large and very cold and contained the compressed acoustic signatures of events spanning approximately four billion years of local galactic history.

The intake desk was staffed by a technician named Prael — a compact, methodical person with short grey hair who had been at the intake desk for as long as Sable had been diving, possibly longer, and who had the air of someone who had seen enough unusual specimens to be genuinely interested in unusual specimens rather than unsettled by them. The desk itself bore the marks of long tenure: a small geode bookend holding a row of physical reference manuals upright, a framed image of a rocky coastline Sable did not recognize propped against the monitor stand, and a mug with a chip in the rim that Prael clearly preferred to its replacement.

Prael was looking at the classification flag on their screen when Sable and Thresh arrived. She could tell from the angle of their body — leaned slightly forward, chin supported on one hand — that this was the expression of someone looking at something that didn't fit the existing organizational schema and finding this more interesting than alarming.

They looked up. *The system has been trying to classify the anomaly since your dive profile uploaded at 11:47,* Prael signed, their colony-standard language slow and broad but readable. *It keeps returning the flag. I ran the scan three times. Crystal density is not registering.*

She signed: *What does that mean?*

*Density measurement requires the system to calculate the compression ratio — how much acoustic data is stored per unit of crystal matrix. For every fossil we've ever processed, this is a finite number. High for stellar events, lower for local impact signatures. The anomaly's compression ratio comes back as —* Prael paused. Their hands reorganized into the configuration for a technical qualifier. *The number is not finite.*

Sable looked at the intake display. The classification field still blank. The flag still reading MANUAL REVIEW REQUIRED.

*Not finite,* she signed.

*The system interprets it as a calculation error. I'm treating it as a classification anomaly pending specialist review from the central Archive.*

*When does the specialist arrive?*

*Four to six standard days.*

She looked at the capture pod, now resting in its intake cradle behind the desk, transferred to the Archive's internal handling system. The green status light had followed it from the *Meridian*. The crystal matrix stable. The fossil contained.

*Has anything unusual happened in the facility since the pod transferred?*

Prael tilted their head. The expression was not quite confusion — more the look of someone who had been asked a question that required more careful consideration than it superficially seemed.

*No,* they signed, after a moment. *Why?*

She held their gaze for a second. Then: *Document everything. Any deviation from standard readings in the vault's environmental monitoring. Anything the system flags, however small.*

Prael nodded, slowly, their expression shifting into something more careful.

She signed *thank you* and turned away from the desk.

Thresh fell into step beside her as she moved toward the Archive's main atrium. She was halfway down the intake corridor when she became aware that someone was standing at the far end.

He was standing still in the way of someone who had chosen to stand still rather than arrived at it — a deliberate stillness, an administrative posture, the stance of a person who managed things. He was wearing Archive administrative grey, the cuffs of which had the particular softness of fabric washed many more times than administrative clothing typically survived — the uniform of someone who had been in this role long enough to wear through its institutional crispness. He was approximately fifty-five years old. He had the kind of face that had arrived at its current configuration through a long series of careful decisions, and the eyes in it were watching her with an attention that was precise and held and did not shift to Thresh.

She knew, before Thresh touched her arm and spelled the name against her skin with one finger — M-A-R-E-T — that this was the administrator.

## Maret

His name in the colony's administrative register was Tomás Maret, Senior Curator, Pan-Systemic Archive — Coda Station. She had seen his name on paperwork. Archive administrators did not, as a rule, come to intake corridors to meet dive crews off the dock. They received summary reports. They approved valuations. They occasionally appeared at premium fossil auctions to represent the Archive's institutional interests. They did not stand at the end of corridors at shift change, waiting.

He did not try to sign. He spoke to Thresh, and Thresh's hands translated in the slight lag of live interpretation.

*He says welcome back from the dive. The classification anomaly flagged in the intake documentation came to his attention and he wanted to introduce himself directly.*

She signed to Thresh: *Tell him the intake technician has submitted a specialist request and the fossil is secured in the vault. Standard protocol.*

Thresh translated. Maret listened. His expression did not change. He spoke again.

*He says standard protocol is appropriate. He says he'd like to know one thing — whether the anomaly's signal presented as structured or as ambient during capture.*

She looked at Maret.

The question was wrong. Not factually — structured versus ambient was a real classification axis in acoustic fossil taxonomy, a distinction between wavefronts generated by discrete events and those produced by continuous atmospheric or environmental processes. It was a legitimate question. But it was not a question a Senior Curator asked a dive crew off the dock the same day as the capture. It was a question a specialist asked after reviewing signal analysis data, which did not exist yet because the classification system had been unable to process the fossil. To ask it now meant Maret already understood enough about the anomaly to know that structured-versus-ambient was the relevant axis. To ask it now meant he had a prior model.

She held his gaze for a long moment.

Then she signed to Thresh: *Structured. Highly structured. Tell him the precise characterization is in my post-dive notation and will be available in the full report.*

Thresh translated. Maret received this. His expression remained exactly what it had been — and it was in that exact consistency, in the absence of any micro-response to the word *structured,* that she read the confirmation. He had already known. He had asked to hear her say it, to have her confirm his model against her firsthand account. He was checking. Whatever he knew, or believed he knew, he was now one data point more confident in it.

He spoke again. Thresh translated: *He thanks us for the prompt intake. We're stood down from active dive rotation pending the specialist review. Full pay continuation. He says we deserve the rest after a heavy harvest.*

She kept her expression neutral. *Tell him we appreciate it.*

She walked past him. She did not look back.

In the atrium, the colony's ambient vibrations surrounded her — the familiar composite signature of four thousand people and their systems and their air processors and their elevators and their fossil refinement equipment, all of it communicated through the structure of the station into the soles of her boots and the palms of her hands where they touched the handrail on the atrium stairs. Known. Mapped. Forty-seven dockings deep in her body's memory.

She climbed the stairs to the residential level and did not think about the one thing she was thinking about, which was the expression Maret had not had when she said the word *structured.*

## The Photograph

Her quarters on Coda were small and precisely arranged in the way of spaces occupied by someone who spent most of their working time in a five-meter dive cabin and had never seen the value of spreading out. A sleeping platform, a desk, a compact sanitation unit, a single shelf. The shelf held four things: a technical manual for the Mark IV dermal transducer array that she had read cover to cover twice and kept for reference, a water glass that lived in the same position for reasons she had never examined, a rectangular piece of matte black composite approximately thirty centimeters by twenty that she had bought at a market on a transit station three years ago because it had a texture she liked — a fine grain, consistent, neither warm nor cold — and a photograph.

The photograph was printed on physical paper, which was unusual enough in the current era to count as an affectation, though she had not thought of it that way when she acquired it. It showed two people standing on the observation deck of a transit hub, backlit by the hub's illuminated arrival board, faces slightly overexposed by the camera's compensation for the bright background. The person on the left was her. The person on the right had been her mother, who had died four years ago in a pressure system failure aboard a refinement barge she should not have been on because she was three months from retirement.

The photograph was the only image she had of her mother's face that she trusted completely. Her memory of her mother's face was reliable — she had studied it sufficiently — but visual memory degraded in ways she could not fully track. The photograph did not degrade.

She had never heard her mother's voice. She had never heard anyone's voice. But she had a phantom memory — had always had it, as long as she could remember — of something she could not name, something that lived in the part of her nervous system that the transducers occupied, a warmth and a texture she associated with her mother in the way she associated everything with sensation. She knew it was neurologically impossible. She had looked it up, twice, in different medical databases, at different points in her adult life. The auditory cortex of a person born profoundly deaf did not process auditory information. The phantom was a misfiled signal, a processing artifact, something her brain had categorized incorrectly in early development. This was the clinical explanation and she had accepted it.

She looked at the photograph now — set her bag on the desk, sat on the edge of the sleeping platform, looked at the shelf with the four things in their four positions — and she felt the transducers running warm, and she thought about the shape that had pressed through her nervous system with the character of *intention,* and she thought: *misfiled signal.*

She looked at the photograph for a long time.

Then she went to the sanitation unit and ran cold water over her hands for approximately ninety seconds — a habit she had developed for recalibrating fingertip node sensitivity after a dive, the thermal contrast helping the nodes reset their baseline — and came back and sat on the sleeping platform and closed her eyes and ran a manual diagnostic through the transducer array. Seven spinal nodes, ten fingertip nodes. The spinal nodes were still warm. The fingertip nodes were at normal operating temperature following the recalibration, except for the two on her right hand flagging reduced sensitivity, already noted and filed.

She lay down. She did not expect to sleep quickly but she was tired in the specific way of someone whose nervous system had processed more information in a single thirty-seven-minute trawl than it usually processed in a week, and she was asleep before she had time to catalogue the sensations of the sleeping platform's familiar pressure distribution.

She did not know how long she slept.

She came awake because something changed.

Not a vibration. The sleeping platform's pressure distribution was the same. The ambient vibrations of the colony's systems were the same. The air against her skin was the same temperature it had been when she lay down.

What had changed was the shelf.

She was looking at the shelf before she had fully decided to open her eyes, some pre-cognitive process having already flagged the thing and directed her attention to it. The four objects were in their four positions. The technical manual. The water glass. The composite rectangle. The photograph.

The photograph was not the same.

She sat up. She moved to the shelf. She picked up the photograph and held it at the angle that gave her the best view under the room's low ambient lighting, and she looked at it carefully — the way she had looked at the condensate droplet on the *Meridian's* port wall, the way she had looked at the rust that bloomed and was not there.

The photograph showed two people standing on an observation deck. Backlit by an arrival board. Faces slightly overexposed.

The person on the left was her.

The person on the right was wrong.

Her hands tightened on the photograph's edges. She felt the pressure through her fingertip transducers — her own grip, registering as data, and beneath the data something that was not data at all. A contraction in her chest, brief and involuntary. She breathed once, deliberately, and loosened her fingers.

Not absent — the figure was still there, same height, same posture, same slight angle of the head she had memorized. The overexposure was the same. But the face — the face she had studied until she trusted it more than her own memory of it — was not her mother's face. It was a face she had never seen. A woman's face, angular and precise, with an expression she could not name because she had never learned to name the expression on the face of someone who was, at the moment the photograph was taken, terrified.

She stood very still with the photograph in her hands.

The figure in the photograph was the same height as her mother. The posture was the same. The clothing was the same clothing her mother had been wearing that day, which Sable remembered because she had been standing next to her. Everything about the figure except the face was her mother.

The face was someone she had never met.

She set the photograph back on the shelf with a precision she was not fully in control of — placing it in the exact position it had occupied, as if precision could hold something in place that was no longer quite where it had been.

She sat down on the sleeping platform and folded her hands in her lap and ran the inventory.

Three symptoms. The condensate inversion: a drop of water falling upward in a column of normal gravity, at eighty-eight atmospheres, immediately post-capture. Aboard the *Meridian,* in the direct physical environment of the capture pod. The rust bloom: approximately eight centimeters of accelerated oxidation on a hull seam, appearing and resolving in approximately two seconds, at eighty-eight atmospheres, immediately post-capture. Aboard the *Meridian,* in the direct physical environment of the capture pod.

The photograph: a substitution of biographical identity in a fixed physical record. Not aboard the *Meridian.* In her quarters. In a room where the capture pod was not, where the fossil was not, where the only thing connecting this room to the lower atmosphere of Vespers was the warmth running through her transducers and whatever she had carried back in her nervous system from the moment the signal returned.

She was in the causal chain. She was the vector.

She sat with this for a while. Then she picked up the photograph again and looked at the face she had never seen, and she thought about the question Maret had asked — *structured or ambient* — and she thought about the precision with which he had not reacted when she said *structured,* and she thought about the phantom memory she had always had and never been able to explain, the warmth and the texture, the misfiled signal that her auditory cortex was not supposed to be able to produce.

The face in the photograph was terrified. Not of anything visible in the frame — the observation deck was ordinary, the other travelers in the soft background blur were ordinary, the arrival board was ordinary. The terror was not situational. It was the terror of someone who understood their situation completely and found that understanding worse than not knowing.

Sable looked at the face for a long time.

She did not know whose face it was. She knew, with a precision she could not yet justify, that this was not a random substitution. She knew, with the same certainty she brought to reading a supernova's shockwave in the spiraling pressure against her finger pads, that whoever this face belonged to had been trying, at the moment it appeared in the frame of her photograph, to tell her something.

She put the photograph face-down on the shelf.

She lay back down on the sleeping platform.

The transducers ran warm. The colony's systems hummed their known frequencies through the floor and the walls and the platform's frame. Outside the colony's hull, the gas giant Vespers moved through its geological time, its crystalline storms trapping the acoustic signatures of the universe's history the way amber traps the remnants of things that have no other way to last.

In the Archive vault on the inner ring's lower level, forty meters of corridors and pressure doors away, a crystal matrix held at controlled temperature maintained the stability of something that was not a sound. The classification system had been trying to process it for six hours. The number it kept returning was not finite.

The warmth in her spine did not diminish.

She did not sleep again.
