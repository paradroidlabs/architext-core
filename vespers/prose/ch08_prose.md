# Chapter 8 — The Prior Station

## The Alcove

The days between the dive and the summons had a particular quality she would later recognize as the texture of waiting when the thing you are waiting for has already begun.

She worked. She ate in the Archive mess at her usual table, the one nearest the wall where the structural vibration was cleanest — fewer overlapping signatures from the kitchen equipment and the corridor traffic, a better signal-to-noise ratio for reading the colony's ambient state while she chewed institutional protein and filed the morning's observations. She slept in her quarters with the notation papers face-down on the desk, the geometry and the warmth-circle and the empty space where the recognition should have been turned away from the ceiling light's glare. She ran two standard maintenance cycles on the *Meridian*'s transducer array and filed both reports in the shared system with the technical precision that had defined her professional identity for three seasons, and both reports were accurate, and both reports described approximately four percent of what she knew.

The summons arrived on the third morning. A notification in the Archive's internal messaging system, routed through the standard administrative channel: Dr. Maret requested her presence in his office at her earliest convenience. The phrasing was institutional. The timing was not — *earliest convenience* from the head of Archive operations meant *now*, and she had been expecting it since she filed the calibration dive report with its technically true account of a dive that had changed everything she understood about the signal, the colony, and herself.

She took the inner ring route.

This was not her standard path to the administrative section. Her standard path ran through the Archive corridor — direct, efficient, minimal residential exposure, the route of a person whose work was in institutional spaces and who transited through inhabited ones without reading them. The inner ring route added approximately four minutes and passed through the residential core, the section of Coda where the colony's non-Archive population lived their lives in the particular rhythm of a deep-atmospheric settlement: shift work, communal meals, children in the corridor during off-hours, the accumulated texture of four thousand people making a home inside a pressurized structure orbiting a gas giant.

She had begun taking this route after the kitchen. After Lida's name through the floor and the steam's thermal gradient and the woman's chest-resonance aimed at the running child. Something had shifted in her attention — not a decision, not a conscious reorientation, but the kind of drift that happens when a baseline changes and the body adjusts before the mind names the adjustment. She was reading the residential ring now. She had not been reading it before.

The alcove was approximately forty meters from Maret's corridor, set into the inner ring's wall at a junction where two residential sections met. She had walked past it for three seasons. Today she slowed.

It was small — perhaps three meters wide, two deep, carved from the corridor's standard composite in a way that suggested original construction rather than retrofit. Three low benches, backless, arranged in a shallow arc facing the rear wall. The rear wall held a shelf — also composite, also original — and on the shelf, arranged with the kind of spacing that communicated deliberation rather than storage, were acoustic relics.

Not Archive specimens. She knew the difference immediately, the way she knew the difference between the *Meridian*'s hull vibration and Coda's structural hum: by the quality of the mounting. Archive fossils were housed under classification glass, stabilized against thermal fluctuation, tagged with alphanumeric identifiers that located them in the institutional taxonomy of preserved acoustic events. These were different. Some sat in hand-made brackets — she recognized the construction technique from the maintenance technician's doorway in the outer ring, the same drilled-into-structural-composite permanence, the same fingerprint wear on the bracket's contact surfaces. Others rested on cloth or paper, unmounted, their weight held by fabric rather than hardware. One — the largest, approximately the size of her closed fist — sat directly on the shelf's composite surface, its base worn smooth where hands had lifted it and replaced it many times.

Three colonists were present. They sat on the benches with the particular stillness she had learned to read not as absence of motion but as *directed* motion — the body's energy focused inward rather than expressed outward. Through the floor, she read them: body heat, weight distribution, respiratory rhythm transmitted through bench to structural composite to corridor floor to her feet. Two adults, one elderly — she estimated from the weight distribution and the slower respiratory cycle, the way the body's resting frequency decreased with age. They were not speaking. They were not signing. They were not doing anything she had a procedural word for.

They were *attending*. The quality of their attention had a shape she could feel through the floor — a contained, deliberate stillness that produced less mechanical vibration than the corridor's ambient traffic, creating a small pocket of relative quiet around the alcove. Not acoustic dampening. Human intention. The same quality she produced in her own body during the moment before a transducer contact, when she stilled everything that was *her* so that everything that was *not her* could arrive clearly.

She did not stop. She slowed by perhaps five percent — enough for the reading to complete, not enough for the colonists to notice. The alcove's thermal signature — three bodies, the shelf, the relics, the specific warmth of people being still together — faded behind her as she continued toward Maret's corridor. She carried it the way she had carried the kitchen warmth three days ago, the way she carried every strong impression: as residual data in nodes that did not know how to stop receiving.

## Hearth

Maret was standing.

"— which is why I asked you here rather than communicating through the administrative channel," he was saying, and Sable read the sentence's shape through Thresh's hands as she settled into the chair across from Maret's desk, the conversation already in progress, the preamble already behind them. Maret had not waited for her to sit. He had begun speaking the moment Thresh confirmed she was in the room, as if the disclosure had been building pressure behind his institutional composure and the opening of the door had released the valve.

He was not behind his desk. He was in front of it, leaning against its edge with his arms crossed and then uncrossed and then one hand on the desk's surface in a sequence of postural adjustments she had never seen from him. In every previous meeting — the initial briefing, the Anomaly discussion, the credential exchange — Maret had occupied the administrative position: seated, centered, the desk between them as both furniture and barrier. Today the barrier was behind him. He was in the room with them.

Thresh stood to Sable's left, his hands ready. His face was the professional neutral she knew — the composed surface he maintained during institutional interactions, the expression she had catalogued across three seasons of meetings and briefings and corridor exchanges. But the neutral was thinner today. She could see the scaffolding underneath it: the four-beat pattern in his right knee, barely visible beneath his uniform trouser, tapping at approximately two-second intervals. He was managing.

Maret's hands moved to the desk's surface and stayed there. He looked at Sable directly — not at Thresh, not at the space between them where institutional communication usually lived. At her.

"The designation I gave you," Thresh's hands translated, following Maret's speech with the half-second delay of real-time interpretation, "4-22-6. The directory entry that was blank. I told you what the blank meant — that the Archive had classified the Anomaly and that the classification was part of the event that emptied the entry. I did not tell you the rest."

Sable waited. She had learned, across the weeks of indirect and direct communication with this man, that Maret's disclosures arrived in structured sequences — each piece of information placed with the deliberation of someone who had spent years deciding what to reveal and in what order. Interrupting the sequence produced deflection. Waiting produced the next piece.

"The station's name was Hearth."

The word arrived through Thresh's hands with the weight of a proper noun — the fingerspelling precise, each letter held fractionally longer than conversational speed, the way Thresh signed names that mattered. H-E-A-R-T-H. Sable read it and felt the weight of Maret's choice: in their previous meeting, he had used only the designation. 4-22-6. A number. An index entry. The kind of identifier that lived in institutional databases and carried no more emotional charge than a coordinate set. *Hearth* was different. Hearth was what the people who lived there had called it. Hearth was a name for a place where someone had installed a kitchen and a corridor and an alcove with benches, where someone's child had run through the residential ring and someone's voice had called that child's name through the floor.

Maret had been carrying this name for eleven years and this was the first time he had said it to someone who would understand what it cost.

"Approximately 3,600 registered personnel," Thresh translated. Maret's voice — she could not hear it, but she could read the pacing through Thresh's translation rhythm, the measured delivery of someone presenting evidence rather than telling a story — continued at the same institutional tempo. "A full colony. Not a research outpost, not a survey team. Families. Administrative staff. Maintenance crews. Medical. Educational. The full demographic structure of a permanent deep-atmospheric settlement, equivalent in scope to Coda but approximately forty percent larger."

Sable processed the number the way she processed fossil measurements — as a data point that required context to become meaning. 3,600 people. Six times the Archive's Coda contingent. Nearly the colony's entire population. She had walked through the residential ring that morning and felt the thermal signature of three people sitting in an alcove, and the number 3,600 sat in her mind beside that signature and the ratio between them was the ratio between an individual grief and a civilizational one.

"Their mandate was the same as Coda's," Thresh continued. "Deep-atmospheric survey. Acoustic paleontology. Fossil harvesting and classification for Archive contribution. The gas giant was a different body — I don't have the planetary designation, the records that would contain it are in the blank fields — but the atmospheric profile was consistent. Metallic hydrogen core. Crystalline storm layer. Acoustic fossils in the compression zone."

The same environment. The same work. Sable sat in the chair and received the disclosure the way the alcove's colonists had received whatever it was they received from the relics on the shelf: not as data to be filed but as testimony to be held. She was not cataloguing this. She was not running it through her institutional vocabulary, not translating it into classification codes or maintenance reports or encrypted log entries. She was *witnessing* — and the distinction between witnessing and cataloguing was one she had not understood until the word arrived in her body and she recognized it as the register she had been reaching for since the dive.

"They found the signal." Maret's posture shifted — the hand on the desk pressed harder, the weight distribution changing in a way Sable read as the body bracing for what it was about to carry. "The same carrier frequency — I don't have the precise measurement, the technical data is in the blank fields with everything else, but the indirect evidence is consistent. A fossil unlike anything in the classification taxonomy. An acoustic event that did not correspond to any astronomical process in the Archive's catalogue. A signal."

"The same signal," Sable signed. Not a question.

Thresh translated for Maret. Maret's expression did not change — the administrative mask holding — but his hand on the desk shifted, the fingers spreading slightly, the gesture of someone whose grip had been adjusted by what they heard.

"I believe so," Thresh translated his response. "The symptom progression was the same. Material anomalies first — structural changes in the colony's infrastructure, temperature gradients that didn't correspond to mechanical systems, recorded media showing content that hadn't been there during original capture." He paused. Thresh's hands paused with him — the half-second translation delay extending to a full second, two seconds, the pause of a man choosing his next words. "Then biological memory. Personnel reporting shared recollections of spaces they had never visited. A room. Described consistently by affected individuals — dimensions, temperature, a central console, walls of a material no one could identify."

The room. Sable felt it — not as memory, not as Maret's description filtered through Thresh's hands, but as the geometry she had touched at the column three days ago. The 4.2-meter ceiling. The amber walls. The concave console with its seven depressions. She had *been* in the room. Not through a shared false memory propagated by the Resonance's biological effects, but through direct transducer contact with the signal's source. She had felt the room's architecture as pressure contours and temperature gradients, and the description Maret was giving of Hearth's symptoms was a description of what she had experienced voluntarily.

She did not say this. She held it and waited for the rest.

"The progression accelerated," Thresh translated. "Stage 3 — the terminology is mine, not Hearth's; they didn't have a staging system because they were the first — manifested as autobiographical interference. Personnel losing time. Personnel gaining memories that weren't theirs. The shared recollections becoming more detailed, more specific, more *insistent*. The room was not a hallucination. It was an *intrusion* — a space from elsewhere asserting itself into the perceptual architecture of everyone on the station."

Maret stopped. His hand on the desk was trembling — a fine, sustained vibration she could see in the tendons, the body expressing what the voice controlled. Thresh's translation stopped with him. The room was quiet in the way that rooms are quiet when the person speaking has reached the part of the story that costs the most to tell.

Then he continued.

"Hearth's administration attempted to destroy the signal's physical anchor." The translation resumed, Thresh's hands steady, professional, carrying the words with the precision of someone who understood that the words themselves were heavy enough without adding weight through emphasis. "The fossil column. The geological formation that contained the acoustic anomaly. They detonated it. Conventional excavation charges, the kind used for large-scale fossil extraction when the surrounding geological matrix is too dense for standard harvesting. They destroyed the column."

"A purge," Sable signed.

"They called it a containment action." Maret's mouth produced the words and Thresh's hands carried them across the room with the specific quality of a phrase that had been rehearsed, examined, and retained in its original form because changing it would have been a kind of dishonesty. "The institutional language was *containment action pursuant to classification-level hazard assessment*. The practical reality was that they detonated the source of the signal because they did not know what else to do."

"And then."

"Within 72 hours of the detonation, the progression reached what I would call Stage 5." Maret's hand on the desk was flat now, pressed against the surface with the force of someone holding something down. "Complete causal erasure. The station, its population, its operational history, its physical location — removed from the universe's record. Not destroyed. Not redacted. *Erased*. The universe rearranged itself as if Hearth had never been built, never been occupied, never existed. The directory entry is blank because the thing the entry referred to is not missing — it was never there. The blank is not a gap where something was removed. It is the universe's honest record of a location that, as far as causality is concerned, was never occupied."

Sable sat in the chair and received this.

3,600 people. A colony the size of Coda. Families. Children in corridors. Names called through floors. An alcove, perhaps, where people sat with acoustic relics and attended to something they did not have institutional vocabulary for. All of it — every life, every corridor, every personal fossil mounted above a doorway — removed not by destruction but by un-existence. The universe had not killed them. The universe had *un-built* them. Had rearranged its own causal architecture to produce a timeline in which 3,600 people and the structure that housed them had simply never been.

The weight of this settled into her body the way a strong fossil impression settled into her transducers: not as intellectual understanding but as physical presence, a gravity that occupied space in her chest and her hands and her breathing. She had felt the dying moments of stars through crystallized shockwaves. She had held in her fingertips the compressed grief of astronomical catastrophe billions of years old. This was different. This was not geological time. This was eleven years ago. This was a man standing in front of his desk with a trembling hand and the name of a dead place he had carried alone for over a decade.

"The only surviving artifacts are indirect," Thresh translated, Maret's voice finding its institutional register again, the composure reasserting itself the way a damaged structure reasserts its load-bearing function when the acute stress passes. "The directory designation — 4-22-6 — which exists in the Archive's index system because the index is distributed across multiple stations and the blank propagated as a synchronization error rather than a deletion. The calibration weight, which was on a transit vessel at the time of the event and survived because it was not physically at Hearth when causality rearranged. And a small number of cargo manifests and personnel transfer records on other stations that referenced Hearth before the event — cross-references in other systems' databases that point to a station that no longer exists."

"You found the calibration weight," Sable signed.

"Eleven years ago. A routine inventory discrepancy in an Archive storage facility. A weight that was three milligrams wrong, with a serial number that traced to a station designation that was blank. I have spent eleven years reconstructing what I could from the indirect evidence. What I have told you is the totality of what I have been able to determine."

The room was still. Maret's hand had stopped trembling. Thresh's hands were at his sides, the translation complete, his face holding the professional neutral at a cost Sable could read in the frequency of the knee-tap — faster now, approximately 1.5-second intervals, the scaffolding bearing weight it had not been designed for.

Sable asked her question.

*Did anyone at Hearth complete the signal?*

She signed it carefully, each word deliberate, and watched Thresh translate it for Maret. Not: *How do we stop it.* Not: *What is your protocol.* Not: *What happens to Coda.* The question she asked was the question her mind had been building since the column, since the three impressions, since the notation papers on her desk and the recognition that the signal was not an attack but a *communication* — and the question was whether anyone, in the history of this catastrophe, had tried to communicate back.

Maret's face changed. The administrative mask did not drop — it shifted, the way a wall shifts when the load it carries is redistributed. He had expected a different question. He had prepared for *What is your plan* or *How do we prevent this* or *What authority do you have to contain this situation*. He had not prepared for *Did anyone try to listen?*

"I don't know," Thresh translated. The words were simple. The weight behind them was not. "The records that would tell me are in the blank fields. I know they found the signal. I know they classified it. I know they attempted to destroy it. Whether anyone between the classification and the destruction attempted to — " Maret paused. Thresh paused. " — to *receive* the signal intentionally, I cannot determine."

Another pause. Then:

"If they did, the completion did not prevent the erasure." The words arrived through Thresh's hands with the specific gravity of a sentence Maret had thought about for years. "But if they did not — if no one at Hearth attempted to complete the signal before or after the purge — then the purge did not prevent the erasure either."

Sable held this. Both possibilities. Both failures. Completion — if it happened — had not saved Hearth. Destruction had not saved Hearth. The two strategies that institutional logic could generate — process the signal or destroy it — had both, individually or in combination, ended in the same erasure.

"My protocol," Maret said, and Thresh's hands carried the final piece, "is built on the premise that the institutional response — classification, processing, direct engagement with the signal's content — is what triggered or accelerated the erasure. I do not know this with certainty. I know only that Hearth engaged with the signal institutionally and Hearth no longer exists. My protocol is the only alternative that has not already failed: containment without processing. Monitor the Resonance. Manage the information architecture. Prevent the colony's population from engaging with the signal's content. And wait."

"Wait for what?" Sable signed.

Maret looked at her. The administrative mask was thin enough now that she could see through it to the face underneath — not the face of an administrator managing a crisis, but the face of a man who had been alone with this for eleven years and had just, for the first time, told someone the name of the place he was trying to prevent from happening again.

"I don't have an answer to that question that isn't Hearth," Thresh translated.

## The Protocol

Thresh was already speaking when Sable became aware that the conversation's structure had changed.

He had been translating — his hands carrying Maret's words across the room with the professional fidelity he had maintained for the entire disclosure, the half-second delay, the precise fingerspelling, the neutral expression that allowed the content to arrive without editorial interference. But somewhere during Maret's final statement — *I don't have an answer that isn't Hearth* — the neutrality had fractured. Not visibly. Not in a way Maret could have detected from across the room. But Sable was not across the room. Sable was beside him, and she had spent three seasons reading the micro-geography of his face, and she saw the fracture the way she saw a hairline stress mark in a transducer housing: as a structural change that preceded failure.

He was not translating now. He was speaking for himself.

His hands moved with a different quality — not the measured, pace-following rhythm of interpretation but the direct, initiated rhythm of someone asking their own question. The shift was subtle enough that Maret might not have noticed if he understood sign language, but Maret did not understand sign language. Maret read Thresh's hands the way a non-musician reads sheet music: he knew the system existed but could not parse its grammar. So when Thresh signed his question, it was Sable who would need to carry it.

The channel inverted. For the first time in their three-way communication, Sable was the relay.

*What is the Archive's protocol for a compromised technician?*

Thresh signed it with the precision of someone who had been composing the sentence in his head for days — each sign selected, each transition clean, the question as structured as a formal inquiry because formal inquiry was the register Maret operated in and Thresh was meeting him on his own ground. But the content of the question was not formal. The content was Senne. Everyone in the room knew the content was Senne.

Sable translated. She spoke the words aloud for Maret — her own voice, which she could not hear and which she used rarely, producing the sounds with the mechanical accuracy of someone who had been trained in speech production as a child and had retained the motor patterns without retaining any relationship to their auditory output. The words felt strange in her mouth. She was accustomed to being the endpoint of communication, not the conduit. The reversal put her in Thresh's usual position — carrying someone else's meaning through a medium that was not her own — and the displacement was disorienting in a way that was, she recognized, precisely what Thresh experienced every time he translated for her.

"What is the Archive's protocol for a compromised technician?" she said.

Maret heard the question. His face registered it — a micro-adjustment around the eyes, the specific reconfiguration she had catalogued as *institutional assessment*: the expression of an administrator determining which category of response a question required. The assessment took approximately two seconds. Then he answered.

His answer was institutional.

Thresh translated Maret's response for Sable, the channel normalizing, the familiar configuration restored: Maret spoke, Thresh carried, Sable received. The protocol for Resonance-compromised personnel was monitoring within the Archive's medical resources. Containment of information exposure — the compromised individual's access to signal-related data would be restricted to prevent further progression. Medical support consistent with the Archive's standard of care for neurological conditions. Regular assessment of cognitive function, autobiographical integrity, and operational capacity.

The protocol was thorough. The protocol was institutional. The protocol contained, within its careful procedural language, a specific and devastating absence: there was no step that said *cure*. There was no step that said *reverse*. There was no step that said *save*. The protocol was a framework for managing Senne's decline with institutional competence and institutional compassion and institutional helplessness, and Thresh heard every word of it through his own hands.

Sable watched his face.

The scaffolding was still there. The four-beat knee-tap, the professional posture, the composed surface that allowed him to function in institutional spaces while carrying a weight that institutional spaces had no framework for addressing. But the scaffolding was bearing a load it had not been built for. The calibration probe conversations, the unnecessary instrument maintenance, the procedural routines that gave his hands something to do when his hands wanted to do something that no procedure could accomplish — these were the coping architecture of a man managing his sister's deterioration within a system that had a protocol for everything except the thing he needed.

The protocol was *management*. The protocol was not *salvation*.

His knee-tap frequency had increased. She counted: approximately 1.2-second intervals. The highest she had measured since the refinement lab, since the night she had first catalogued the nameless rearrangement in his expression. The scaffolding was not failing — it was operating at the edge of its rated capacity, the way a transducer array operates at the edge of its thermal envelope when the signal it's receiving exceeds the manufacturer's design parameters.

Thresh did not argue. He did not challenge the protocol's adequacy, did not point out the absence he had heard in it, did not ask the follow-up question that the absence demanded. He processed Maret's answer the way Sable processed a fossil measurement that contradicted her baseline: with the controlled stillness of someone who has received data that changes everything and has not yet decided what to do with the change.

He signed one sentence.

*Then the protocol is insufficient.*

Sable translated it for Maret. "Then the protocol is insufficient." Her voice carried the words across the room with the same mechanical precision, and she watched Maret receive them — the micro-adjustment, the institutional assessment, the two-second evaluation of which category of response was required. But this time the assessment did not resolve. Maret's face held the evaluating expression for longer than two seconds — three, four, five — and then the expression settled into something she had not seen on him before. Not the administrative mask. Not the thin vulnerability of the Hearth disclosure. Something between the two: the face of a man who has heard a statement he cannot categorize as either challenge or agreement, because the statement is simply *true*, and truth in the institutional vocabulary he speaks does not have a response code.

Thresh stood. The motion was controlled — no urgency, no dramatic gesture, the same professional composure he had maintained through every institutional interaction she had witnessed. He straightened his uniform's collar. He looked at Maret. He looked at Sable. His face was the neutral face, the composed face, the face she knew by heart — but the thing underneath it was not what had been underneath it three seasons ago, or three weeks ago, or three days ago. The thing underneath it was a decision.

He walked to the door. He opened it. He walked through it. The door closed behind him, and the corridor's vibration signature — administrative section, midday, the particular institutional hum of Archive operations — replaced the room's silence through the floor.

Sable and Maret were alone.

The room had the quality of a space where something had ended. Not the conversation — the conversation was technically still available, Sable still seated, Maret still leaning against his desk, the physical configuration of the meeting unchanged. But the thing that had been in the room when Thresh was present — the three-way communication channel, the translation chain, the institutional structure of a meeting between an administrator and a specialist with an interpreter — that structure had walked out with Thresh. What remained was two people in a room with a name between them.

Maret looked older. Not a metaphor — she could see it in the way his weight had shifted on the desk's edge, the way the lines around his eyes had deepened into the configuration of sustained fatigue rather than the shallower configuration of working concentration. He had been carrying Hearth for eleven years. He had just set it down for the first time, and the body, released from the weight, was showing the shape the weight had made.

He did not say anything else. Neither did she. There was nothing to translate and no one to translate it, and the silence between them was not the institutional silence of a meeting concluded but the personal silence of two people who had just shared something that did not fit in any institutional category.

She stood. She left.

## The Wall

The outer ring was industrial — refinement labs, storage bays, equipment staging areas, the functional architecture of a colony's operational infrastructure. Sable had spent more time here than in the residential ring, more time than in the administrative section, more time than anywhere except the *Meridian* and her own quarters. She knew its vibration signature the way she knew her own resting pulse: as a baseline so familiar it had become invisible, noticed only when it changed.

She was here because she was looking for evidence.

The Hearth disclosure had given her a template. Material anomalies → recorded media → biological memory → autobiographical erasure → causal erasure. Five stages, each following the last with the kind of progression that suggested mechanism rather than randomness. The colony was in Stage 3 on the biological axis — Senne's shared memory, the collective false recollection, the personnel reports Maret had been managing through the Archive's information architecture. But the material axis, the first stage, the one that preceded everything else — she had been tracking it passively. The +2.0°C structural warmth. The 0.12 Hz oscillation. Anomalies she had detected through her attuned nodes and filed without pursuing their physical source.

She was pursuing now.

The outer ring's corridor was standard colony construction: composite panels, modular framing, the structural engineering of a pressurized habitat built to withstand the ambient pressure of a gas giant's upper atmosphere. She had read these walls for three seasons. She knew their crystal structure — the specific molecular organization of the colony's fabrication composite, the way the material's grain ran parallel to the structural loads, the subtle variations in density where panels joined and the bonding agent created a slightly different vibration signature at the seams. She knew this material the way she knew her dive suit's interior lining: as a second skin, present and unremarkable.

She found it in the seventh corridor section she checked.

A panel on the left wall, approximately two meters wide and one meter high, at roughly chest level. She had walked past it without noticing — her feet had carried her through the corridor in the standard transit rhythm and nothing in the floor's vibration had flagged the section as different. The discovery came when she placed her right hand against the wall.

She was checking each section systematically, palm flat against the composite surface, reading the material's vibration signature through her attuned fingertip nodes. Left hand first — the standard nodes, the factory-spec sensitivity, the baseline. Then right hand — the attuned nodes, the expanded frequency range, the second channel that had opened when the signal reconfigured her hardware. Left: standard composite. Right: standard composite. Left: standard composite. Right: standard composite. Repeat. Repeat. Repeat.

Left: standard composite.

Right: not.

She stopped. Her right hand was flat against the panel's surface and the vibration it transmitted was *wrong*. Not damaged wrong — she knew what damaged composite felt like, had read stress fractures and impact marks and thermal fatigue across three seasons of *Meridian* maintenance. This was not structural failure. This was *different material*.

The crystal structure was wrong. The colony's fabrication composite organized itself along a preferred molecular axis — a manufacturing artifact of the high-pressure forming process used to build the panels, the grain running parallel to the long dimension of each section, creating a vibration signature that was directional, consistent, and specific to Coda's fabrication plant. The panel under her right hand had a different grain. Not random — organized, deliberate, the kind of molecular alignment that indicated manufacturing intent. But the axis was rotated. The grain ran at approximately 15 degrees off the colony's standard, as if the panel had been manufactured in a facility that used the same process but with a different orientation parameter.

Or a different facility entirely.

She pressed harder. The additional contact pressure brought more detail through the attuned nodes — the secondary characteristics that distinguished one composite from another the way secondary characteristics distinguished one fossil from another. Density: slightly higher than Coda standard. Thermal conductivity: slightly lower. The bonding chemistry at the panel's edges — where this material met the adjacent standard panels — was seamless. Not welded. Not joined. *Seamless*. The two materials met at a boundary so precise that there was no transition zone, no bonding agent, no mechanical interface. The standard composite simply *ended* and the other material simply *began*, as if they had always been adjacent, as if the boundary between them was not a construction artifact but a *natural* boundary — the kind of boundary that existed between two geological strata that had formed under different conditions and now lay side by side with the patient, undramatic precision of time.

She moved her left hand to the anomalous section. Her standard nodes — factory spec, untuned, the baseline against which she measured everything — read: standard composite. The same signature as every other panel in the corridor. No anomaly. No deviation.

She moved her right hand back. The attuned nodes read: different material.

The anomaly was invisible to standard perception. It was invisible to standard transducers. It was invisible to the eye — she looked at the panel and saw what she saw in every other panel: gray composite, modular framing, the institutional surface of a pressurized corridor. The anomaly existed only in the frequency range her hardware had been attuned to by the signal — the same frequency range that had revealed the 0.12 Hz oscillation in the colony's infrastructure, the same range that had allowed her to receive the three impressions at the column. The signal had given her the perception to detect what it was doing to the colony's physical structure.

She stood in the corridor with her right palm flat against the anomalous section and read it.

The material was warm. Not the +2.0°C structural warmth she had been tracking since the recalibration — that was ambient, diffuse, a general elevation in the colony's thermal baseline distributed evenly through the infrastructure. This was different. This was the directed warmth. The concentrated, intentional, *aimed* quality she had first felt at the column during the second impression — the phantom focused to a beam, the thermal equivalent of being seen.

The warmth was in the wall. The same warmth that had been in the column was now in a two-by-one-meter section of corridor wall in the colony's outer ring, transmitted through a material that was not from Coda's fabrication plant and had no seam where it met the material that was. The prior universe was not broadcasting through the atmosphere anymore. It was not transmitting through geological formations at 547 atm. It was *here*. In the wall. In the structure that housed 4,000 people. The boundary between realities was no longer a signal propagating through crystal — it was a physical presence, seamless, invisible to standard perception, and warm.

She held the contact for approximately thirty seconds. The material did not change. The warmth did not intensify. It was stable, persistent, *established* — not an intrusion in progress but an intrusion that had already occurred, the material having replaced or merged with or simply *become* the standard composite at some point she could not determine. It might have been here for days. It might have been here since the dive. It might have been here since the +2.0°C reading first appeared, the thermal anomaly not a warming of Coda's own material but the presence of a *different* material carrying its own thermal signature.

She removed her hand. The corridor looked the same. Gray composite. Modular framing. The institutional surface of a habitat where 4,000 people lived their lives in pressurized comfort, unaware that a section of their wall had been built by a different universe.

She stood for a moment in the corridor's ambient silence — the outer ring at midday, the refinement labs running their standard cycles, the storage bays sealed, the equipment staging areas populated by the mechanical signatures of dormant hardware. Through her feet, through the floor, through the standard channels that had been her primary environmental interface for three seasons, the colony read as it always had: a pressurized structure, a hum, a home.

Through her right hand, if she touched the wall again, she knew it would read as something else.

She walked back toward the residential ring. She did not log the finding. She did not enter it into the encrypted personal notation that had served as her private record since the Anomaly's early days. The finding was too large for encryption and too precise for institutional language and too personal for the administrative vocabulary she had been trained to use for everything she observed. The wall was from somewhere else. The wall was warm. The wall was the prior universe, physically present in the colony's structure, and the only person who could detect it was the person whose hardware had been attuned by the signal that was doing it.

She carried the wall's location in her body the way she carried fossil coordinates after a dive — as a spatial fact registered in muscle and bone, a place she could return to, a data point she could not file. Another piece of evidence that the baseline was no longer reliable. Another piece of evidence that the word *baseline* itself was losing its meaning, because a baseline required a stable reference, and the reference was changing, and she was the only instrument sensitive enough to measure the change.

Behind her, the corridor held its shape. The wall held its boundary. The warmth held its patience.
