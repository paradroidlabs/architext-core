# Chapter 3 — Transmission

# No Sleep

She hadn't slept again. The colony's systems cycled through their overnight protocols — she could feel the shift in the atmospheric processor frequency as they moved to reduced load, a slightly slower pulse than the daytime operation, which she had catalogued somewhere around her fifth docking without meaning to — and she lay on the sleeping platform and ran the inventory again. Not because it produced new conclusions. Because running the inventory was what she did when she had a problem she couldn't yet solve: she enumerated the known data until the shape of the unknown became cleaner by contrast.

Three symptoms before docking. One since. The rate was not accelerating yet, but she had no baseline for what acceleration would look like because she had no prior model for any of this. She had Maret's prior model, or the implication of one, which was not the same thing as having it.

At 03:11 she stopped running the inventory and sat up.

The photograph was still face-down on the shelf. She did not turn it over.

She dressed. She put on her boots and laced them by feel in the dark, which she always did because the alternative was turning on the overhead light and she had never been able to fall back asleep after overhead light. She picked up her jacket from the desk and was at the door before she had consciously decided to go anywhere, which meant some lower-level part of her processing had already determined the next step and was simply waiting for the rest of her to arrive at it.

She needed to tell Thresh.

## Thresh

The residential corridors on Coda's inner ring ran in a long arc, the curvature of the structure giving each hallway a slight upward slope in one direction and downward in the other that she had learned to use as navigation by feel. Thresh's quarters were seventeen doors down from hers in the downhill direction — she had never counted deliberately, but she had walked it enough times that her feet knew the distance before her conscious mind did. The corridor was empty. The overhead lights had shifted to night mode, a dimmer amber than the daytime setting, and the colony's vibration signature was different at 03:00 — quieter, slower, the particular stillness of a place that was not quite asleep but had stopped pretending to be fully awake.

She stopped at his door and pressed her palm flat against it — the standard attention signal they had developed for situations where knocking was inadequate and lights-on was too much. The door transmitted the vibration in three short pulses, which he would feel through the floor of his quarters.

Forty seconds. Then the door opened.

Thresh looked at her with the specific expression of someone who had not been fully asleep. He was still dressed — or dressed again, she couldn't tell — and his hair was doing something irregular on the left side, and his eyes moved from her face to the empty corridor behind her and back, taking inventory the way she had been taking inventory for the past four hours.

His hands: *How long have you been awake?*

*Since I slept,* she signed.

A pause. Then: *Come in.*

His quarters were slightly larger than hers, occupied by the particular entropy of a person who was meticulous about his tools and unconcerned about everything else. Equipment cases were stacked in a precise column along one wall. The desk held three tablets and a partially disassembled depth gauge and two coffee cups that were not clean. The sleeping platform had a jacket on it, which confirmed the dressed-again hypothesis. He moved the jacket and gestured toward the platform and she sat on the desk chair instead, because the desk chair put her back to the wall and she wanted to be facing the room.

He sat on the platform and looked at her and waited. Thresh was good at waiting. It was, she had decided years ago, the most important quality in someone who worked with her — not patience in the vague motivational sense, but a genuine absence of the need to fill silence, to prompt, to rush the conclusion. He understood that she was always thinking, that the pause before she signed was not hesitation but composition, and he had never once in three seasons tried to complete her sentences.

She signed: *I am the vector.*

She let him read that before she continued. His expression did not change much, but she saw the slight shift around his eyes that meant he was processing something that had confirmed rather than surprised him — that he had already been in the vicinity of this conclusion and she had just handed him the last piece.

She laid it out in sequence. The four symptoms. Their locations. The relationship between the fossil's position and the first three, the break in that relationship for the fourth. The transducers running warm. The logical implication.

*The Resonance is not radiating from the fossil,* she signed. *Or not only from the fossil. Something from the capture transferred through the hardware into my nervous system and I brought it to the colony in my spine.*

Thresh's hands: *The warm transducers.*

*Yes.*

*Since capture.*

*Yes.*

He looked at his hands for a moment, which was what he did when he was organizing a response that had more components than usual.

*Then it's not contained in the vault,* he signed. *Whatever the Archive thinks it's holding, it's not holding all of it.*

*No,* she signed. *It is not.*

He stood up. Not agitated — Thresh did not get agitated; he simply moved from sitting to standing when sitting was no longer the correct posture for the situation. He went to the desk and picked up one of the tablets and turned it toward her. She read the screen.

It was a maintenance notification from the Archive's environmental monitoring system, logged at 02:47 local time. She read the notation: *VAULT TEMPERATURE ANOMALY — SECTOR 4C — LOCALIZED COLD SPOT, -12°C BELOW AMBIENT, DURATION 3 MINUTES 41 SECONDS — SELF-RESOLVED — FLAGGED FOR TECHNICIAN REVIEW.*

Vault temperature anomalies were not common, but they were not unprecedented — the refrigeration system ran in zones and occasional fluctuations occurred at zone boundaries. Twelve degrees below ambient was outside the normal fluctuation range. Three minutes forty-one seconds was long enough to be significant. Self-resolved meant no technician had intervened.

*When did you get this?* she signed.

*It routes to all active crew with recent Archive intake. Standard notification.*

She handed him the tablet back. *Prael was supposed to be monitoring.*

*They probably were. Self-resolved means the system logged it and moved on.*

She thought about Prael's expression when she had said *document everything* — the slight shift from interested to careful. Prael was documenting. Whether Prael had flagged it upward was another question.

*We need to go to the outer ring,* she signed.

Thresh looked at the tablet. Then at her. His hands: *Why?*

She didn't have a clean answer. What she had was a pattern-recognition operating below the threshold of enumerable data — that the vault anomaly and the photograph and the warm transducers were pointing at something that was not staying in one place.

*Because the fossil didn't stay in one place,* she signed. *And neither did I.*

## Refinement Labs

The outer ring at 03:30 was not empty. The acoustic fossil refinement labs ran a continuous processing cycle — the crystalline matrix of newly harvested fossils required temperature management that didn't respect shift changes, and the technicians who worked the overnight rotation were a specific type of person: methodical, self-contained, comfortable with the particular isolation of being responsible for things that had taken billions of years to form and could be destroyed in seconds by a temperature excursion or a handling error.

The refinement labs occupied a crescent of the outer ring's lower level, separated from the main corridor by pressure doors that were always slightly ajar during active processing to allow the ventilation system to manage the temperature differential between the refrigerated lab interior and the corridor. She and Thresh walked the corridor in the amber of the night lights, and she was running the ambient vibration signature against her memory of previous night-shift walks on this corridor when she felt something change.

Not a dramatic change. A frequency — not from the floor, not from the walls, but from behind the pressure door of lab seven, through the door itself as she passed it because the door was slightly ajar and she was close enough to the gap.

She stopped.

The vibration from lab seven was wrong. Not wrong in the way of a mechanical problem — she knew the signature of equipment malfunction, the arrhythmic pulse of a failing component, and this was not that. It was wrong in the way the photograph had been wrong: internally consistent, precisely the wrong thing, with the quality of a substitution rather than a failure. Something in lab seven was doing exactly what it was supposed to do. It was doing it in a direction she did not have a technical term for.

She put her hand on Thresh's arm. He stopped. She pointed at the door.

He stepped to the gap and pressed two fingers against it, reading the vibration in the way she had taught him years ago — not with her precision, but with enough sensitivity to confirm that something was happening.

His hands: *The processing array?*

She nodded.

*Running backwards?*

She looked at him. He had felt it without her framing it. She held up one hand — *wait* — and pushed the pressure door open.

The lab interior was lit by work lights positioned over the processing array — a series of glass-enclosed chambers along the far wall, each containing a fossil in its crystal matrix, each connected to the array's temperature management and acoustic analysis systems by a network of sensor filaments. The overnight technician was standing approximately three meters from the array with her back to the door. She turned at the change in the room's vibration when the door opened, and her face was doing something.

She was not injured. She was not in distress in any physical sense. But her expression had the quality of someone who had been presented with something outside their operational framework and had not yet found a framework to replace it with.

Sable crossed to the monitoring desk. The overnight log was on the screen. She read it.

The processing array had been running a standard acoustic analysis on three fossils — all routine harvest material brought in two weeks ago, all previously classified, awaiting completion of the Archive's provenance documentation. The analysis system read the frequency content of each fossil's crystalline matrix and translated it into standard acoustic notation: a visual spectrogram that human analysts could review for classification confirmation.

All three fossils were displayed on the monitoring screen. Two of them looked exactly as they should — the familiar spectrographic patterns she had seen on a hundred post-harvest documentation sets. Stellar formation on the left, asteroid impact on the right.

The middle fossil was displaying its spectrogram in reverse.

Not the spectrogram reversed — the spectrogram was the same shape it had been two weeks ago, the same pattern. But the time axis of the display was running backward. The interval markers were moving from right to left rather than left to right, the analysis time counter decrementing rather than incrementing.

The fossil's acoustic content was running backward. Not playing backward — the crystal matrix was stable, the physical object unchanged. The analysis system's read of the content was reversed. The system was reading a fossil that contained a forward-direction acoustic event as if the event were running in reverse, as if time within the crystal were flowing in the opposite direction from time outside it.

She looked at this for a long moment. Then she looked at the overnight technician.

The technician's name badge read SEVI. Her expression said she had been looking at this for approximately as long as it would take to convince yourself twice that you were not misreading the display, and that after the second confirmation she had not known what to do and had stood very still instead.

Sable signed to Thresh: *Tell her to log it exactly as she's reading it. Don't correct for it. Don't restart the analysis. Log the output as received.*

Thresh spoke. Sevi looked at Sable, and her expression shifted from the helplessness of a person outside their framework to the relief of a person given a procedurally correct thing to do. She went to the monitoring desk and began logging.

Sable looked at the middle fossil through the glass of its chamber. The crystal matrix was intact. The status indicator was green. The fossil was, by every physical measure, unchanged.

The time axis on its spectrogram continued to run backward.

She turned to Thresh. *How many labs are running overnight analysis?*

His hands: *Probably four or five. Labs three, seven, nine, and twelve usually run continuous.*

She looked at the pressure door. Three other labs.

*We should check them,* she signed.

---

Labs three and twelve were normal. Lab nine had a single fossil displaying a spectrogram in a language — she had no better word for it — that the analysis system did not recognize. Not reversed, not corrupted, not a display error. The system was reading the fossil's acoustic content correctly, translating it accurately into visual notation, and the notation was a pattern of frequency and amplitude that did not correspond to any event type in the Archive's classification database. Not a known event type rendered unusually. Something else. Something the database did not contain a category for.

She logged it and left it running and told the technician in lab nine the same thing she had told Sevi: log exactly what you see, do not correct for it, do not restart.

Walking back through the corridor toward the inner ring, she was running the implications in sequence. The Resonance had reached the refinement labs. The fossil vault was in the inner ring, forty meters of corridors away from the outer ring. Between the vault and the labs, the only physical link was the cargo transfer system, which moved raw harvest material, not fossil vault contents.

The Resonance was not moving through the physical infrastructure.

She was the only person who had been in the lower atmosphere of Vespers in the past twenty-four hours. She was the only person who had been in direct contact with the anomaly. She was the only person with warm transducers.

*But I wasn't in the refinement labs tonight,* she signed to Thresh, as they reached the pressure door between the outer and inner ring. *I was in my quarters. And then I was with you.*

He stopped walking. She could see him working through the same implication.

*The Resonance doesn't require your proximity anymore,* he signed. *You were the initial vector. Now it's spreading on its own.*

She nodded. This was the conclusion she had been building toward for the past hour, and it was worse than the vector hypothesis, which had implied containment. Self-propagating meant it no longer required her. It had already made the jump.

Stage one: inanimate matter. Stage two: recorded media. The fossils in the refinement labs were recorded media — ancient acoustic recordings stored in crystal. The reversal of time axes. The unclassifiable frequency pattern. Precisely what Stage 2 looked like.

Stage two had already arrived.

She pushed through the pressure door into the inner ring. She needed to find Thresh's sister.

## Senne

Senne worked the analysis division of the outer ring's refinement facility, which meant she was a day-shift employee who would not begin her rotation for another four hours. She lived three floors above the residential level in a section of the inner ring built during the colony's second expansion phase — the ceilings were fractionally lower there, a consequence of the structural modifications required to add the upper residential levels to the existing framework, and Sable had learned to notice the change in the vibration profile when she crossed into this section because the lower ceilings altered the resonance characteristics of the corridor in a way her transducers registered as a slight increase in ambient texture density.

The vibration profile was the same as always. This was either reassuring or insufficient.

She pressed her palm to Senne's door in three short pulses. Thresh stood behind her and slightly to the left, which was where he always stood when he was uncertain how a situation was going to resolve — not defensively, not blocking, but positioned to move in any necessary direction.

The door opened in approximately twenty seconds, which meant Senne had also not been fully asleep.

She was slightly taller than Thresh and three years younger, with the same cast to her jaw and the same quality of stillness, but where Thresh's stillness was operational — the stillness of a person who moved precisely when movement served a purpose — Senne's had an extra layer of deliberateness to it, the stillness of someone who had spent years learning to be in rooms without dominating them. She had chosen analysis work, Thresh had told Sable once, because she found the decay signatures beautiful — the way time recorded itself in the slow degradation of high-frequency harmonics. *It's like reading tree rings,* she had said, *except the trees were stars.* She communicated in a mix of the colony standard and the modified cabin language she had learned from Thresh, which meant Sable's comprehension of her was higher than it was for most people on the station.

She looked at Sable, then at Thresh, then at the corridor behind them, then back at Sable.

Her hands: *Is this about the fossil?*

Sable looked at her. *What do you know about the fossil?*

*I know the classification system can't read it. Prael told me.* A pause. *I was at the intake desk when the pod transferred. I helped with the physical relay from the cargo system.*

Sable felt something in her chest that was not a transducer reading. She kept her expression level. *When you say helped with the transfer — how close were you to the pod?*

Senne's hands: *I handled the pod. Both hands. Standard cargo relay. Why?*

She looked at Thresh. He was looking at his sister. She could see the specific rearrangement happening in his face — the moment of understanding arriving and being received and being held very carefully, the way you held something you knew was fragile.

He looked at Sable. His face was asking a question he was not ready to sign yet.

She looked back at Senne. *Has anything seemed wrong today? Anything in your quarters, your equipment, your routine that didn't match what you expected?*

Senne considered this with the seriousness of a person who understood that the question was not casual.

*There was a recording,* she signed, slowly. *A reference recording I use for calibration. I've had it for two years. A stellar formation event, class three, standard reference material. I ran it this morning for a calibration check and the output was the same recording, same event, same frequency profile — but the decay signature was wrong. Acoustic decay is how we estimate fossil age. The older the event, the more the high-frequency components have degraded. This recording's decay signature read as something much older than it should have been. The same recording I've run two hundred times, but the system read it as if the event happened much further back.*

Sable kept her hands still. *How much further back?*

*The system wouldn't give me a number. It flagged the decay signature as out-of-range for the known classification.* Senne paused. *I thought it was a calibration error. I was going to run a hardware diagnostic in the morning.*

*Don't correct for it,* Sable signed. *Log exactly what the system reports. Don't restart, don't recalibrate.*

Senne looked at her for a moment with the expression of someone revising their understanding of the scope of a problem they had thought was small.

*What is it?* she signed.

Sable thought about the shape that had pressed through her nervous system with the character of intention. She thought about the face in the photograph, the terror of someone who understood completely.

*I don't know yet,* she signed. *I'm working on it.*

They stayed for another hour. Senne made tea and distributed the containers — she said the act of handling them helped her think — and Sable held hers without drinking it and felt the warmth against her palms, which were already running their own warmth from the transducers, and she thought about Senne handling the capture pod with both hands and she thought about the decay signature reading wrong and she thought about Stage 3 and tried to calculate how much time she had before the colony's short-term biological memory started showing the same distortions the fossils were already showing in the labs.

She did not arrive at a reassuring number.

She and Thresh left at 05:20. The colony's vibration signature was beginning to shift toward its day-shift profile — the freight elevator making its first full run of the morning, the atmospheric processors cycling up, the outer ring labs beginning their transition. She walked with Thresh as far as the junction between their corridors.

His hands, at the junction: *I'll check on her every shift.*

She signed: *Yes.*

*She doesn't know what this means.*

*No.*

*But you do.* His hands stayed up — not finished. *When does documenting become doing something?*

She held his gaze. It was the first time in three seasons he had questioned her method rather than her conclusion. She thought about what she knew, which was: a five-stage escalation, a classification system outputting non-finite numbers, a vault temperature anomaly, a photograph with the wrong face, refinement fossils with reversed time axes, a calibration recording that had aged ten billion years overnight, and a man in administrative grey who had asked the right question in the wrong moment because he already knew the answer. She thought about what she did not know, which was: how to make any of that knowledge into something that would bring Senne's lost time back.

*I know enough,* she signed. *Not enough yet.*

He held her gaze for a moment. The expression she had seen on his face for three years — the one that meant he trusted her and wished he didn't have to — was still there, but it had acquired an edge, a thinness, the look of something that was being spent faster than it was being earned.

Then he went down his corridor and she went down hers and the colony shifted into its morning frequency and the transducers in her spine ran warm all the way to her door.

## The Access Log

She was at her desk, writing up the night's observations in the systematic notation she used for post-dive logs, when the tablet lit with an incoming access notification.

She had set up the notification after Maret stood her down from the rotation — a simple system flag on her post-dive report file that would alert her to any external access. Not standard procedure. Something she had done in the same quiet, unhurried way she had told Prael to log everything: not because she had proof of a specific threat, but because the pattern of available data made preparation rational.

The notification read: *FILE ACCESSED — POST-DIVE REPORT REF. 7744-K — 03:47 LOCAL — CREDENTIAL: ARCHIVE ADMINISTRATIVE ACCESS, LEVEL 5 — USER ID: [REDACTED].*

She looked at this for a moment. The access had occurred at 03:47 — eighteen minutes after she had left her quarters to go to Thresh's. The credential was Archive administrative, Level 5, which was senior curatorial access. There were exactly two Level 5 credentials active on Coda Station.

One of them was Maret.

The user ID was redacted, which was not a standard feature of the access log system. She had reviewed her access logs after previous dives and the user ID had always been visible. The redaction was not a system default. It was a manual modification, applied to this specific access event, after the fact.

Maret had read her post-dive report at 03:47 while she was in Thresh's quarters, and had then gone back into the access log and redacted his own credential.

He had not, however, removed the access event itself. He had not deleted the notification trigger she had set. He had not removed the timestamp, or the credential level, or the file reference.

She looked at the notification for a long time.

There were two explanations. The first was that he had made an error — that the redaction had been partial, that he had removed his name but not noticed the remaining data. She did not find this convincing. A man who asked the right question with the wrong timing and held his expression perfectly still when she said *structured* was not a man who made careless errors.

The second explanation was that he had left it deliberately. That the redaction was not an attempt at concealment but a message: *I know you have monitoring active. I know you will see this. I am telling you that I am reading your reports and I am telling you that I am not telling you who I am, and I am leaving you the negative space of that choice to interpret.*

He was talking to her. Through the access log. In the language of what he had not done, which she was, unfortunately, fluent in.

She closed the notification. She opened a new file. She began logging the night's observations in full — the refinement labs, the reversed time axis in lab seven, the unclassifiable frequency pattern in lab nine, Senne's miscalibrated decay signature, the access notification with its redacted credential.

She logged it all with the same precision she brought to post-dive notation. When she was done she looked at what she had written and she thought: *this is what Stage 2 looks like.* Recorded media. The fossils running backward in their chambers, reading their age wrong, playing in a direction the universe did not currently support.

She thought about how long she had before Stage 3.

Then she closed the file and set it to encrypted local storage only — no Archive network access — and went to make coffee, and stood in the small kitchen area feeling the morning vibration of the colony against the soles of her feet, and thought about Maret reading her report at 03:47 and the face in the photograph and the word *structured* and the silence that had followed it.

She thought: *he knows what it is.*

She thought: *I need to know what he knows.*

She thought: *I need to know it before Stage 3.*

The coffee was ready. She drank it standing up, looking at the shelf with the four objects in their four positions. The photograph still face-down. She did not turn it over.

She was almost certain that if she did, the face would still be there.

She was less certain than she would have liked that it would still be terrified, and not something else.
