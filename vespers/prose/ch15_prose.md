# The Weight

Sable’s hands were black with synthetic grease up to the wrists. 

The *Meridian* was bleeding its internal organs onto the deck of the civilian dive bay. She knelt beside the open port-side chassis, using a heavy torque-wrench to uncouple the secondary acoustic filtration baffles. The ship was rated for 600 atmospheres. The metallic hydrogen core of Vespers existed at a pressure so profound that standard metric measurements failed; it was a theoretical crush-depth where gas compressed into liquid metal. 

To survive the descent, the *Meridian* needed Archive-grade carbon-silicate pressure armor. But even with the armor, Sable knew the calculus. The metallic hydrogen layer would compress the hull at over ten million atmospheres of pressure. They were not building a ship to survive; they were building a ship to buy enough seconds to complete the transmission before they were crushed into a singularity. Because of the thrusters' limits, they had to strip four hundred kilos of operational weight just to compensate for the plating.

She pulled the baffle free. It hit the deck plates with a hollow, ringing thud that transmitted through Sable’s boots. 

Thresh was working in silence on the starboard side, dismantling the commercial fossil-retrieval winch. His luminescent signs had been brief and purely operational all morning. *Cut here. Pull this. Check the manifold.* He was moving with a grim, unrelenting velocity. The institutional clock was ticking—fourteen days before central command arrived—but their true clock was Senne. Senne was fading. Days, not weeks.

They were building a coffin, and they were racing to finish it.

***

The vibration of the dive bay shifted.

Sable paused, her hands resting on the exposed wiring of the *Meridian*. The ambient rumble of the civilian docks—the clatter of chain-hoists, the heavy footfalls of trawler crews, the hum of low-tier atmospheric processors—faltered. It didn't stop entirely, but the cadence broke. 

She turned and looked down the main transit corridor.

Director Maret was walking onto the civilian docks. 

He was not wearing his administrative coat, but his posture carried the full, terrifying weight of the Pan-Systemic Archive. Behind him, two heavy-load automated freight handlers tracked his steps, their treads vibrating low and thick against the deck. 

On the pallets were slabs of dull, matte-black material. Compressed carbon-silicate plating. Restricted military-grade hull armor.

Around them, the social texture of the docks crystallized into a tense, wary stillness. Civilian trawler mechanics, covered in the same synthetic grease as Sable, stopped their maintenance. A woman calibrating a sonar net lowered her tools. A crew of four hauling a rusted haul-winch went totally still. They were a working-class ecosystem that survived by avoiding the Archive’s eye. Now, the eye was standing in their bay, delivering restricted technology to a scrap-diver.

Maret stopped at the edge of Sable’s berth. The freight handlers lowered the pallets with a pneumatic hiss that sent a sharp tremor through the floor.

Maret looked at the dismantled *Meridian*. He looked at Thresh. Finally, he looked at Sable. 

He did not speak. He did not sign. He simply unclipped a data-pad from his belt, keyed in a final authorization code, and placed the pad on top of the black plating. 

Then he turned and walked back the way he came. 

He was abandoning the purge, but he was not escaping his complicity. He was funding the suicide dive.

***

Three hours later, the bay was blinding with actinic light.

Thresh was running a high-heat plasma torch along the port-side seam, welding the first massive slab of carbon-silicate armor to the *Meridian's* hull. 

Sable stood back, her hands pressed flat against the outer bulkhead of the dive bay to monitor the structural integrity of the weld through the metal. The heat of the plasma torch was intense, registering against her facial nodes as a dry, biting pressure. 

But it was the acoustic resonance of the metal that caught her attention.

The Archive plating was not civilian steel. It was hyper-dense, designed to deflect acoustic weapons and extreme pressure shears. As the plasma torch bit into the edge of the plate, the metal screamed. To Sable’s attuned nodes, the vibration was a complex, multi-tiered harmonic.

And beneath the scream of the weld, she felt it.

The 0.0037 Hz carrier frequency was still pulsing through the colony’s deck plates, driven by the atmospheric processors. The intense, hyper-localized acoustic vibration of the Archive metal being superheated by the plasma torch was interacting with the ambient carrier. 

It was an acoustic collision. A combinatorial reaction.

Sable closed her eyes, shutting out the glaring blue light of the torch, and focused entirely on her fingertips. The interference pattern blossomed in her mind. It was dense, intricate, and heavily structured. 

She mapped the frequency modulation. The pressure variables. The decay rate.

It locked. 

Coordinate 9.

The phoneme hit her nodes with the clarity of a physical strike. It encoded SPATIAL ORIGIN. The sentence was establishing exactly where the speaker was standing relative to the geometry of the universe when the broadcast began. It was not a set of physical coordinates in a three-dimensional grid; it was a positional anchor relative to the expansion of the cosmos itself. 

Nine coordinates. Three remaining.

The signal was using the preparation of the dive as an instrument, pulling the remaining phonemes out of the act of building the vessel. Sable watched Thresh lower the torch. Senne was deteriorating too fast to wait for safe testing protocols for the final three coordinates. They would have to dive now, and derive numbers ten, eleven, and twelve during the descent itself.

***

The medical unit was quiet. 

Thresh sat in the molded plastic chair beside Senne’s bed. He had washed the grease from his hands, but the skin around his cuticles was still stained dark. 

Senne was at baseline. Her eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling tiles, but they were vacant. The catastrophic fog of Stage 4 erasure had settled deeply into her architecture. She did not know what room she was in. She did not know who was sitting beside her.

Thresh didn't look for a clarity window. He didn't wait for her eyes to track him. He knew the eight-second bursts of lucidity were becoming rarer, consumed by the demands of the signal using her biology.

He reached out and took her hand. Her skin was warm, a low 38.4 degrees. 

Sable stood outside the door, her hand resting on the frame. Through the floor, she read the micro-tremors of Thresh’s posture. His heartbeat was slow, heavy, and wet. 

Thresh squeezed his sister’s hand. He did not sign. He simply sat in the quiet, committing the weight of her fingers to his memory.

He was going down into the metallic hydrogen core. The *Meridian* was armored, but it was still a one-way trip. He was not coming back. He was leaving her to save her, trusting that completing the signal would reverse the erasure before the fog became permanent.

He leaned forward, his forehead resting against the edge of her mattress.

Sable looked away, staring down the long, empty corridor of the medical wing. Nine coordinates down. Three to go. The crush depth was waiting.
