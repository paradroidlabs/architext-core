# Chapter 4 — The Gap

## The Missing Station

The Archive's public classification index was not a document most people on Coda had reason to access. It was a reference system — a master catalogue of every acoustic fossil ever processed by any Pan-Systemic Archive station across all operational colonies, organized by station designation, processing year, and fossil classification type. It existed primarily for provenance verification: when a fossil changed hands commercially, when a researcher needed to confirm the origin station and processing date of a specimen, when an auditor ran the annual compliance review. It was, in the way of all large institutional record-keeping systems, simultaneously comprehensive and invisible — a structure so complete that its completeness was itself unremarkable.

Sable accessed it from the public terminal in the Archive's atrium lobby at 07:14 local time. The atrium was beginning to fill with the morning shift — she felt the accumulating footfall through the floor tiles, the gradual thickening of the colony's vibration signature as bodies moved through the space. She had chosen the public terminal rather than her personal tablet because the public terminal accessed the index through the Archive's external-facing system, which logged queries differently than the internal network. She did not want this search appearing in the same system Maret was monitoring.

The index loaded as a structured hierarchy. Top level: station designations, each identified by a numeric code in the Archive's standard format — a three-part identifier consisting of sector number, atmospheric band, and sequential station number within that band. Coda was 7-31-4: sector seven, thirty-one-atmosphere band, fourth station established in that pressure range. She had never examined the designation system before. She examined it now because she was looking for a pattern, and patterns required understanding the structure they existed within.

She expanded the directory. Forty-three station designations were listed. She knew, from general knowledge acquired over three seasons within the Archive's framework, that the Pan-Systemic Archive operated stations on eleven gas giants across four star systems. Forty-three stations was consistent — approximately four per planet, matching the economic model of acoustic trawling: each planet's atmospheric bands supported a limited number of viable harvesting zones.

She began reading the index systematically. Not searching — reading. The way she read a fossil's frequency profile: cataloguing the full spectrum so that any anomaly would present itself against the baseline.

Station 1-15-1. Entries from 2089 to present. Classification types spanning the full taxonomy: stellar, planetary, galactic, local, exotic. Processing volumes consistent year to year. Complete.

Station 1-15-2. Entries from 2094 to present. Same pattern. Complete.

She read through the first twelve stations in sector one. Then the nine stations in sector two. Then sector three's seven stations. Each one the same: an unbroken chronological sequence of classification entries, every year accounted for, every fossil type represented, the institutional record of decades of systematic harvesting rendered in the tidy columns of a well-maintained database.

Sector four had six stations. She read through stations 4-22-1 through 4-22-5 without incident. Station 4-22-6 was listed in the directory. Its designation appeared in the index key — same format as all others, same three-part numeric code, same hierarchical position in the directory tree.

The entry was empty.

Not redacted. She checked this carefully. A redacted entry in the Archive system carried specific markers: a classification flag reading RESTRICTED, an access-level indicator, an audit trail showing when the restriction was applied and by what authority. She had seen redacted entries in the index — two of them, in sector one, both flagged with standard restricted markers and annotated with the credential level required for access. The system for redaction was bureaucratically thorough. It left traces by design, because institutional redaction was itself a form of record-keeping: the record of having restricted a record.

Station 4-22-6 had none of this. The designation existed in the directory. The entry it pointed to contained nothing. No classification data. No processing years. No fossil types. No station name. No footnote, no annotation, no flag of any kind. The directory listed forty-three stations and forty-two of them had entries and one of them was a designation pointing at a space where data should have been and was not.

She looked at the surrounding context. Station 4-22-5 had entries running from 2097 to present. Station 4-23-1 — the first station in the next atmospheric band — had entries from 2103 to present. Between them, 4-22-6 occupied its position in the sequence with the particular emptiness of a chair left at a table where the person who sat in it had not excused themselves but had simply never existed.

She cross-referenced. She entered the designation 4-22-6 into the Archive's general search system — the public-facing catalogue that covered not just classification indices but station logs, personnel records, maintenance schedules, atmospheric survey data, anything tagged with a station designation. The search returned zero results.

Not *no matching records.* Zero results. The system did not even return a null-result summary page. It returned the search interface, blank, as if the query had not been executed.

She ran the search again. Same result — or same absence of result. She tried a partial search: 4-22. This returned entries for stations 4-22-1 through 4-22-5. Not 4-22-6. The system acknowledged the existence of five stations in atmospheric band twenty-two of sector four. The sixth existed only in the directory key and nowhere else in any accessible Archive document.

She sat at the public terminal and looked at the screen and thought about what kind of absence this was.

Redactions were removals. Someone decided a record should not be visible, and applied a restriction, and the restriction itself became part of the record. This was institutional behavior. It left fingerprints.

This was not a removal. There was nothing to have removed. The station designation existed — it had been assigned, it occupied its correct sequential position — but no data had ever been attached to it, or no data remained that the system could reference. It was the difference between a locked door and a wall where a door should have been. One implied something behind it. The other implied that the space behind it had never been built.

Or had been un-built.

She thought about Stage 5 of the Resonance escalation curve she had been constructing in her encrypted logs. Causal erasure. Objects removed from photographs, then from memory, then from physical space. She had projected Stage 5 as a theoretical endpoint — the logical terminus of a process that had so far moved through matter, through recorded media, and was now approaching biological memory.

Station 4-22-6 looked like the aftermath of Stage 5. Not suppressed. Not classified. *Erased from the causal record.* A station that had once existed on a gas giant somewhere in sector four, atmospheric band twenty-two, sixth in its sequence — and whatever had happened there had ended not with the station being shut down or its records being sealed, but with the station ceasing to have been.

The index number remained because index numbers were structural — they were part of the system's architecture, not its content. You could erase the data a designation pointed to, but the designation itself was woven into the directory's framework. It was a seam in the wall where the door had been.

She logged the designation, the empty entry, and the search behavior in her encrypted local file. She closed the terminal. She stood up and walked through the atrium toward the residential ring, and the colony's morning vibration surrounded her — the day shift fully engaged now, the atmospheric processors at full load, the freight elevator running its regular cycle — and she carried the shape of the absence with her the way she carried the warmth in her transducers: as data that had no framework yet, but would.

## Senne's Lost Time

Thresh was in the corridor outside her quarters. He was standing with his back to the wall in the posture she associated with waiting — not the restless waiting of impatience but the deliberate stillness of someone who had arrived at a position and was holding it. He had been standing there for some time. She could tell because his weight was settled evenly on both feet in the way that took approximately ten minutes to develop, the body finding its resting configuration.

He saw her and his hands came up before she had fully stopped walking.

*Senne,* he signed.

One word. His hands dropped and came up again, and the second sign was not a word but a modifier — the particular stillness after the word that she had taught him years ago in the *Meridian's* dive cabin, the one that meant *wrong in a way I don't have language for yet.*

She looked at his face. The expression she had catalogued — the one she had filed as *trusting her and wishing he didn't have to* — was there, but it had been modified. Something had been added to it. Something she did not want to name because naming it would require acknowledging what it meant, and what it meant was that the clock she had been running in her head since the refinement labs had reached a number she had hoped it would not reach yet.

She signed: *Tell me.*

His hands: *She was at her workstation. Analysis lab, day shift prep. Activity log shows continuous input from 06:30 to 06:47. Then nothing. No inputs for seven minutes. At 06:54 she became aware again. She was standing. She had been sitting. She doesn't know when she stood up.*

Sable held his gaze. *Where was she? During the seven minutes.*

*She doesn't know. She says—* He paused. His hands reorganized. She recognized the pause: he was translating something he had heard into their sign system, and the translation was not mapping cleanly because the thing he had heard did not fit the categories their language had been built to express. *She says she was in a room.*

*A room she recognized?*

*No. A room she has never been in. She described it to me. She was very specific.* His hands moved through the description with the careful precision of someone relaying technical data: *High ceiling. Much higher than any room on Coda. The walls were a material she couldn't identify — not metal, not stone, not composite. She said it looked like it had been grown rather than built. The surface had a grain to it, like wood, but the color was wrong — a dark amber that was too uniform to be organic. The light came from everywhere and nowhere — ambient, no fixtures, no source she could locate. There was a surface in the room, a desk or console, but the interface was nothing she recognized. No screen, no keys, no haptic surface. Just a smooth expanse of the same amber material, slightly concave, like a bowl that had been flattened almost but not entirely flat.*

Sable's hands were still. She was holding the description in her mind the way she held a fossil's frequency profile — complete, uninterpreted, waiting for the analysis to begin.

*And there was a sound,* Thresh signed.

She watched his hands.

*She said—* He stopped again. This pause was different. This was not a translation problem. This was a problem with what he was about to say. *She said there was a sound in the room, but she didn't describe it as a sound. She said it was a pressure. She said it was like something pressing on the inside of her skull. Not painful. Just present. She said she could feel it in her teeth.*

Sable's transducers pulsed. A single, brief flare of warmth along the spinal array, not from any external source.

Senne was hearing. Senne had heard sounds her entire life. Senne would describe a sound as a sound — as something with volume and pitch and timbre, the way hearing people described sounds. She would not describe a sound as a *pressure* unless the thing she had experienced was not, in fact, a sound in any way her auditory system was equipped to process.

Or unless the memory she was describing had been encoded by something that did not perceive sound the way humans did. Something that perceived sound as pressure. As shape. As geometry.

The Resonance was not inserting false memories in the colony's native sensory language. It was inserting memories in *its own.*

*How is she now?* Sable signed.

*Shaken. Functional. She went back to her workstation after she told me. She said she didn't want to stop working because stopping would mean thinking about it.* His jaw was set in the particular configuration she recognized — holding himself together through the discipline of reporting. *I told her to log it. She's logging it.*

*Good.* Sable signed. Then: *It's not just her.*

Thresh looked at her.

*I need to check,* she signed. *Come with me.*

## The Survey

The mess hall on Coda's inner ring served the transition between night and day shifts — a large, low-ceilinged space with long composite tables and a food preparation area along the far wall that Sable had always found slightly too warm, the heat from the cooking surfaces creating a localized thermal differential she felt as a subtle gradient across her face and hands when she entered. The room was approximately half full. Morning shift workers eating before their rotations. Night shift workers finishing meals before sleep. The normal traffic of a working colony.

She stood at the entrance and read the room the way she read a fossil field — not looking for anything specific, but cataloguing the whole so that the specific would reveal itself.

Three people at the second table from the door were sitting with a particular quality of stillness. Not the stillness of conversation, not the stillness of eating, but the stillness of people who had recently been somewhere else in their minds and had not fully returned. One of them — a woman in processing coveralls, perhaps forty, her hands wrapped around a drink container she was not drinking from — was looking at the table surface with an expression Sable recognized. It was the expression she had seen on the overnight technician Sevi's face in lab seven: the face of someone who had encountered something outside their operational framework and had not yet found the replacement framework.

The second person, a younger man in maintenance grey, was touching his own forearm with the fingers of his other hand, pressing lightly, as if confirming the arm was solid. The gesture was small, almost unconscious, repeated at intervals of approximately eight seconds.

The third was staring at nothing. Not at the wall, not at the table, not at the food in front of them. At a point in space approximately forty centimeters in front of their face, as if something were there that only they could see.

Sable moved through the mess hall. She did not approach the three. She observed. She continued to the corridor beyond and turned left toward the residential section, and in the first sixty meters of corridor she saw two more.

A technician at a junction point who stopped walking, looked at her own hands — turned them over, examined the palms, then the backs, then the palms again — and resumed walking with the careful deliberateness of someone who had just verified that their body was still their body.

A child.

The child was sitting on the floor of the residential corridor approximately twenty meters from the junction, legs crossed, a drawing tablet propped on her knees. She was perhaps seven or eight. She was drawing with the focused intensity of a child who had been given a task by her own mind and was executing it with the seriousness children bring to things adults have not yet taught them to dismiss.

Sable stopped. She looked at the drawing.

The child's hand moved in confident, unhesitating strokes across the tablet surface. The image was crude in the way of all children's drawings — proportions approximate, perspective intuitive rather than calculated — but the proportions were specific. A room. A tall room, the ceiling drawn at the very top of the tablet's display area, the walls tapering slightly inward in the child's interpretation of height. The walls were colored in a dark amber that the child had achieved by layering brown over yellow. The surface of the walls had been given texture — small parallel lines, drawn with care, suggesting a grain. In the center of the room, a low shape that could have been a table or a console, drawn as a slightly concave rectangle. No doors. No windows. A light source indicated by yellow radiating from the ceiling and walls simultaneously, the child's solution to the problem of ambient illumination with no fixture.

Sable crouched beside the child. She looked at the drawing for a long time.

Then she touched Thresh's arm, and when he crouched beside her, she signed: *Ask her what this is.*

Thresh spoke. The child looked up at him with the unsurprised expression of a child interrupted in important work.

She answered. Sable watched Thresh's face as he listened, and she saw the moment the words arrived — a small, involuntary contraction around his eyes, a micro-expression she had catalogued years ago as the one he made when something confirmed a fear he had been trying not to have.

He turned to Sable. His hands: *She says it's the room where the sound lives.*

Sable looked at the drawing. The dark amber walls. The concave console. The ambient light.

*Ask her when she saw it.*

Thresh asked. The child answered.

*She says she didn't see it. She says she was there. This morning, before breakfast. She was in her room and then she was in that room and then she was in her room again and her mother was asking if she wanted fruit.*

*How long was she there?*

The child considered the question with the gravity of someone taking it seriously.

Thresh translated: *She says she doesn't know. She says the room doesn't have time in it.*

Sable stood up. The corridor's vibration signature was unchanged — the colony's morning pulse, regular, industrial, known. The child returned to her drawing. She was adding something to the console surface now — a small shape that Sable could not interpret from her angle, but the child's hand moved with the assurance of someone drawing something they had seen clearly.

Over the next hour, Sable and Thresh moved through the colony's common spaces with the systematic thoroughness of an epidemiological survey. She did not ask questions directly — she could not, without Thresh — but she observed, and when she identified someone displaying the particular stillness, the hand-checking, the thousand-meter stare, she sent Thresh to speak with them while she watched from a distance that allowed her to read his signing.

Six confirmed. Six people who had experienced moments of discontinuity in the past twelve hours — gaps ranging from approximately three minutes to approximately eleven minutes, during which they had been somewhere else. A room. The same room. High ceiling. Amber walls with a grain like grown material. Ambient light with no source. A concave surface.

None of the six had been to the refinement labs. None had been near the vault. None had any connection to the dive crew or the anomaly capture. Two were residential — a maintenance worker and a food preparation specialist. One was a child. One was a dock worker who had been on Coda for nine years. Two were analysis technicians who worked in sectors that had shown no Stage 2 symptoms.

The Resonance had crossed into biological memory. It was writing the same place into the minds of unconnected people across the colony, regardless of proximity to the fossil, regardless of exposure pathway. Stage 3.

Sable stood in the corridor outside the mess hall and looked at Thresh and he looked at her and neither of them signed anything for approximately fifteen seconds, which was a long time in their language.

Then she signed: *I need to go to my quarters.*

His hands came up fast. *To document.*

It was not a question. The way he signed *document* carried the same edge she had heard in his hands the night before at the corridor junction — the question he had asked then, *when does documenting become doing something,* still open, still unanswered, and now sharpened by six confirmed cases and a child who drew rooms that did not exist.

*To think,* she signed. *Thinking is what I do before I act.*

*Six people,* he signed. *One of them is seven years old. One of them is Senne.* His hands paused, then dropped, then came up one more time — smaller, closer to his body, the way he signed things he did not want to be signing. *Think fast.*

She went.

## The Room

The four objects were in their four positions on the shelf. Manual. Water glass. Composite rectangle. Photograph, face-down.

She sat on the desk chair and opened her encrypted file and began transcribing the survey data. Six confirmed cases. Symptom profile consistent across all subjects: temporal discontinuity, spatial displacement to a shared location, no memory of transition, no physical after-effects. Onset within the past twelve hours — which placed the earliest cases approximately concurrent with the refinement lab anomalies she and Thresh had documented overnight.

She logged the room description from each source. Compiled them into a composite. High ceiling — all six agreed. Dark amber material with organic grain — five of six; the sixth described it as "brown, like something old." Ambient light, no fixture — all six. Concave surface, console or table, no recognizable interface — four of six had noted it; two had not mentioned it but had not contradicted it. Sound described as pressure — three of six, including Senne. The others had not mentioned sound. The child had said the room "doesn't have time in it."

She read the composite description. She read it again.

Then she stopped reading it and sat very still in the desk chair and felt something happen that she did not have a technical category for.

The transducers flared. Not from an external signal — no vibration through the floor, no pressure change in the room, no contact with any surface. The seven spinal nodes fired simultaneously, producing a wash of warmth that started at the base of her skull and moved downward through the array in a pattern she recognized.

She recognized it because she had felt it every day of her life.

The phantom. The warmth and texture she had always associated with her mother. The neurologically impossible sensation she had carried since before she could remember, since before the transducers were grafted, since before she had any framework for what it was — a processing artifact, a misfiled signal, a ghost in the nervous system of a person born deaf whose auditory cortex had reorganized itself around a stimulus that should not exist.

It was here now. Not as a memory. As a *sensation,* present tense, unfolding in real time through hardware that was not receiving any external input. The transducers were producing the phantom from inside. They were playing it back.

And the phantom was not a sound.

Her hands, resting in her lap, began to tremble. Not the visible shaking of panic or the rhythmic oscillation of fear — a fine, interior vibration, as if the transducers' signal had found the muscles of her fingers and was resonating through them at a frequency her body could not absorb. She pressed her palms flat against her thighs. The trembling continued for approximately four seconds. Then it sank below the threshold of sensation — not stopped, but lowered, the way a signal drops below detection range without actually ceasing. She kept her palms pressed down for a moment longer. Then she released them.

It had never been a sound. She had called it that because she had no better word — the phantom *voice,* her mother's *voice* — but what it actually was, what it had always been, was a place. A space with specific dimensions, specific materials, a specific quality of light. A room. High ceiling. Walls of something grown, not built, dark amber with a grain that ran in long parallel lines. Light that came from everywhere. A surface that curved inward like a bowl almost flattened.

She was in the room. Not physically — she was in her quarters, she could feel the desk chair, the floor, the colony's vibration through the structure — but the phantom had depth now, had spatial extension, had architecture. It was not a signal. It was an environment. And she had been carrying it since birth.

The composite description from the survey was the same room.

She understood this with a precision that did not feel like deduction. It felt like recognition. The way her body recognized the *Meridian's* drive frequency after three seasons — not because she had studied it, but because it had been pressed into her long enough to become part of her own signature.

The Resonance was writing this room into the colony's memory. It was the shared false memory the escalation curve predicted for Stage 3 — a place none of the colonists recognized, experienced simultaneously by unconnected people. But for Sable, it was not false. It was not new. It was the thing she had always known and never been able to name.

She had not been infected by the Resonance when the anomaly came through the fractal-mesh nets. She had been *activated.* The neural pathway the transducers occupied — the pathway that had always run warm, that had always carried the phantom — was not a processing artifact. It was a receiver. It had been tuned to this frequency before she was born, before the transducers were grafted, before she had any conscious framework for what she was receiving.

The anomaly in the capture pod had not created her connection to the Resonance. It had amplified a connection that already existed. She was not the vector because the fossil had contaminated her hardware. She was the vector because her hardware had mapped onto a pre-existing signal — a signal she had carried in her nervous system since the beginning of her nervous system, a signal that had been waiting for something powerful enough to bring it above the threshold of conscious perception.

This was why the anomaly had felt like intention. Why the voice had registered as a voice and not as noise. Why the geometric shape had pressed through her transducers with the character of *meaning* rather than the character of random signal.

She was the audience. Whatever the voice in the fossil had been saying — whoever had been mid-sentence at the moment of capture, panicked, urgent, trying to communicate something before the window closed — they had been speaking to *her.* Not to the *Meridian's* sensor array. Not to the fractal-mesh nets. To the neural pathway that had been carrying their room, their place, their phantom, for the entire duration of Sable's life.

She sat in the desk chair with her hands in her lap and the transducers running warm — warmer than they had been since the capture, warmer than the post-exposure diagnostic had flagged, warm with the heat of a signal that was no longer phantom but present — and she looked at the shelf with the four objects in their four positions.

The photograph was still face-down.

The face in the photograph. The unknown woman with the expression of complete terror. The face that had replaced her mother's.

She thought about the room. She thought about the face. She thought about a being from a dead universe, mid-sentence, panicked, reaching across the gap between one cosmos and the next with the only tool available — a voice trapped in crystal, a grief compressed into geology — and she thought: *the face in the photograph is yours. You put it there. You put yourself where my mother was because you have been where my mother was — in the place in my nervous system that I have always associated with love — and the terror on your face is not the terror of the anomaly. It is the terror of someone who knows they are about to stop existing and is trying, desperately, to be heard before they do.*

She did not turn the photograph over.

She sat with the knowledge for a long time. The colony's vibration hummed through the floor and the walls and the frame of the chair. The morning shift continued outside her door. Four thousand people lived on this station and at least six of them had been inside a room that did not exist in this universe, and Sable had been inside it her entire life without knowing what it was.

She thought about Maret. She thought about the empty entry in the classification index. Station 4-22-6, which had a designation and no data, which existed in the directory key and nowhere else, which had been not redacted but erased from the causal record.

She opened her post-dive report file.

## The Date

The file was as she had left it — her post-dive documentation, the observations she had logged, the encrypted local backup running separately. The access notification from Maret's 03:47 visit was still in her alert queue, with its redacted user ID and its visible timestamp and credential level.

She added a single entry to the report. She typed it into the observation log, in the standardized notation format used for post-dive anomaly documentation:

*CROSS-REFERENCE NOTE: Station designation 4-22-6. Classification index entry empty. No associated records in Archive general catalogue. No redaction markers. Designation present in index key only.*

She added nothing else. No analysis. No interpretation. No mention of the survey, the shared memory, the phantom, the face in the photograph. Just the designation number and the observation that it pointed at nothing.

She saved the file.

She set the tablet on the desk and waited.

The colony's vibration signature continued its morning pattern. She felt the freight elevator complete a cycle — the particular shudder she had catalogued as the car reaching the lower dock and reversing. She felt the distant thrum of the atmospheric processors at full load. She felt the faint, irregular pattern of footfall in the corridor outside her door.

The access notification arrived at 08:31. Three minutes after she had saved the file.

She opened the notification.

*FILE ACCESSED — POST-DIVE REPORT REF. 7744-K — 08:31 LOCAL — CREDENTIAL: ARCHIVE ADMINISTRATIVE ACCESS, LEVEL 5 — USER ID: T. MARET, SENIOR CURATOR.*

No redaction. His full name. His full credential. The timestamp precise to the second.

He had dropped the redaction. The last time, he had removed his identity and left the metadata — a message in the language of what he chose not to hide. This time, he had presented himself completely. She read the shift: the first communication had said *I am watching and I am not telling you who I am.* This communication said *I am watching and I am telling you exactly who I am.*

She had changed the terms by entering the station designation. He had responded by changing his.

She opened the report file itself, expecting to find it as she had left it.

It was not as she had left it.

Below her entry — below the cross-reference note, below the station designation and the observation of its empty record — there was a new line. Same notation format. Same standardized font. Entered at 08:31, the same timestamp as the access event.

Maret had written in her report.

The entry read:

*2167-04-11.*

A date. Nothing else. No annotation, no classification code, no credential stamp. Just ten characters in standard date format.

1. Approximately fifty-nine years ago.

She looked at the date for a long time.

April 11, 2167. She did not know what had happened on that date. She did not need to. The date was the answer to the question she had asked by entering the station designation. She had said: *I found the gap in your records.* He had said: *here is when the gap was made.*

Station 4-22-6 had existed until April 11, 2167. On that date, or beginning on that date, whatever had happened there had ended with the station's removal from the causal record. Not destroyed — destruction left debris, left insurance claims, left memorial notices. Removed. Un-built. The wall where the door had been.

Maret knew the date because Maret had access to information that did not exist in the public record — information that had survived the erasure, or information that had been preserved separately from the systems the Resonance had reached. He was approximately fifty-five years old. If the event had occurred fifty-nine years ago, he had not been alive for it. But he had inherited the knowledge. He had been given, or had found, the records of a previous occurrence, and he had carried that knowledge to Coda, and he had been waiting.

He had asked *structured or ambient* because he had known, from the prior event, that the Resonance produced structured signals. He had stood Sable and Thresh down from dive rotation because he had known, from the prior event, that proximity to the capture crew was a variable that needed to be controlled. He had read her post-dive report at 03:47 because he had known, from the prior event, that the diver's observations would be the first reliable documentation of the Resonance's escalation.

He was not suppressing the anomaly out of bureaucratic reflex. He was executing a protocol. A protocol derived from the previous occurrence — from the event that had ended with a station being erased from causality.

The protocol had not saved station 4-22-6.

Sable closed the report file. She closed the access notification. She sat in the desk chair and felt the transducers running warm along her spine and looked at the shelf where the photograph lay face-down and thought about a date fifty-nine years in the past and a room with amber walls and a child who had said *the room doesn't have time in it* and the expression Maret had not had when she had said the word *structured.*

She had the shape of it now. Not the full pattern — not the mechanism, not the solution, not the origin of the voice or the nature of the room or the identity of the face in the photograph. But the shape. The outline of the thing she was dealing with, drawn in the negative space of what was missing.

A previous Resonance event. A previous station. A previous escalation through the same stages she was now documenting — inanimate matter, recorded media, biological memory — and a previous Stage 5 that had ended with the station's erasure from the causal record. Maret knew. Maret had a protocol. The protocol had failed before.

And Sable had been carrying the Resonance's room in her nervous system since birth, which meant the Resonance had been reaching into this universe for longer than fifty-nine years, longer than the transducers, longer than the Archive's classification system, longer than the colony. The phantom was older than she was. The signal predated the receiver.

She picked up the tablet. She opened her encrypted local file. She began writing.

The transducers ran warm. The colony hummed. Outside the hull, the gas giant Vespers turned in its orbit, its crystalline storms holding the compressed acoustic history of four billion years in their frozen depths, and somewhere in that archive of deep time, something that had once been a voice continued to grieve in a language the universe had not been built to contain.
