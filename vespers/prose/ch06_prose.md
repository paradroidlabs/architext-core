# Chapter 6 — Baseline

## The Recalibration

The diagnostic cradle held her right hand at a thirty-degree angle, fingers separated by calibration spacers, the transducer contact points aligned with the cradle's sensor array. The *Meridian*'s maintenance bay was cold — atmospheric recyclers ran six degrees below colony standard in the engineering sections, a thermal management choice Sable had stopped noticing two seasons ago — and the cradle's titanium surface conducted that cold directly into her palm. She had been sitting here for eleven minutes. The diagnostic was on its second full cycle.

The first cycle had returned results she did not understand.

Standard transducer diagnostics measured four parameters: signal sensitivity (the minimum detectable amplitude across the node's rated frequency range), response latency (the delay between acoustic input and haptic output), harmonic fidelity (how accurately the node reproduced the frequency structure of the incoming signal), and thermal baseline (the node's resting temperature absent any input). For the seven spinal nodes and eight of the ten fingertip nodes, the results were within manufacturer specification — the same numbers, within statistical noise, that she had recorded at her last annual diagnostic fourteen months ago. The hardware was performing as designed.

The two right-hand fingertip nodes — index and middle, the pair she had flagged after the Anomaly capture as showing reduced sensitivity — were not within specification. But they were not degraded. The response curves had shifted. Sensitivity in the standard acoustic frequency range — the band that covered everything from geological compression events to stellar nucleosynthesis signatures, the entire vocabulary of the fossil record — had decreased by approximately fourteen percent. She had noted this after the capture and filed it as potential thermal damage from the overload event, pending recalibration.

The recalibration data told a different story.

The sensitivity had not decreased across the board. It had *redistributed*. The fourteen percent lost in the standard band had reappeared — amplified, concentrated — in a narrow frequency window centered on 0.0037 Hz. A frequency so low it was barely a frequency at all: one full oscillation every four and a half minutes. In the standard acoustic taxonomy, nothing lived there. Fossil signatures occupied frequency ranges from fractions of a hertz to hundreds of kilohertz. Geological events sat in the low single digits. Stellar phenomena ranged higher. The 0.0037 Hz band was empty — too slow for any catalogued astronomical process, too fast for tectonic or gravitational-wave phenomena at the scales the Archive's instrumentation was designed to detect.

It was not empty. It was the Anomaly's frequency.

Sable recognized it the way she recognized the *Meridian*'s drive signature or the colony's atmospheric processor cycle — not as a number retrieved from a log but as a sensation her body had recorded and filed in the architecture of her nervous system. 0.0037 Hz was the slow, deep pulse that had underlain the Anomaly's geometric assault during the capture. Beneath the burning angles and the total-node overload, beneath the shape that had pressed into her like a language made of pressure, there had been this: a carrier wave. A foundation frequency. The thing the signal rode on, the way a voice rides on breath.

Her right-hand nodes had tuned themselves to it.

She ran the diagnostic a third time. The cradle hummed — she felt it as a low, diffuse warmth spreading from the contact points through the metacarpal bones of her hand, the diagnostic array cycling through its stimulus sequence. The results populated on the display: the same curves, the same redistribution, the same narrow spike of elevated sensitivity at 0.0037 Hz. Three identical runs. Statistical noise could not produce three identical anomalies. This was not damage. This was not degradation. This was adaptation.

The nodes had been exposed to the Anomaly's carrier frequency during the capture event and had restructured their response profiles to prioritize it. They were now more sensitive to the Anomaly's specific frequency band than they had ever been to any frequency in the standard fossil range. The hardware had not broken. It had *learned*.

She withdrew her hand from the cradle and held it in front of her, palm up, fingers slightly spread. The two affected nodes sat beneath the skin of her index and middle fingertips — invisible from the outside, detectable only through the faint warmth they generated during active reception. They were warm now. Not from the diagnostic — from the ambient. They were receiving at 0.0037 Hz. Passively. Continuously. The Anomaly's carrier frequency was present in the colony's background vibration, and her recalibrated nodes were picking it up the way a radio tuned to a specific station picks up the station's broadcast even when no one is listening.

She opened her maintenance log on the *Meridian*'s engineering console and began composing the entry. She typed: *Right-hand fingertip nodes (index, middle) — diagnostic confirms response curve redistribution, not degradation. Sensitivity peak has migrated to 0.0037 Hz band, consistent with Anomaly carrier frequency. Nodes show attunement to—*

She stopped. Deleted the last word.

*Attunement.* The word implied intention. It implied that something — the nodes, the Anomaly, the interaction between them — had acted with purpose. That the redistribution was not a passive consequence of overload but a *directed* change, a tuning, the way an instrument is tuned to a frequency by someone who wants to hear that frequency. She could not defend the word. Transducer nodes were hardware. They did not attune. They responded to stimuli within their rated parameters and, when exposed to stimuli outside those parameters, they were damaged, degraded, or — in the rarest cases documented in the manufacturer's technical literature — they underwent thermal restructuring that permanently altered their response profiles. The manufacturer's term was *parametric drift*. It was a known failure mode. It was not attunement.

She retyped: *Nodes show frequency drift, non-standard, concentrated in 0.0037 Hz band. Recommend monitoring. No immediate operational impact on standard-range fossil detection.*

She submitted the entry. She closed the console. She sat in the cold maintenance bay with her warm right hand resting on her knee, and the word she had deleted sat in her mind with the specific, persistent quality of something that was technically wrong and descriptively precise. The nodes had not drifted. They had been tuned. Something had tuned them. She could not say this in a maintenance log because maintenance logs did not have a field for *something restructured my hardware with what appears to be intention*, and because saying it would require her to identify what the something was, and she was not yet prepared to write in an official document what she increasingly suspected: that the Anomaly had not just passed through her transducers during the capture. It had *configured* them. It had found two nodes in her fingertips and adjusted their sensitivity to match its own carrier frequency, and it had done this with the precision of a technician calibrating an instrument, and the instrument it had calibrated was her.

She stood. She placed both hands flat on the maintenance bay's workbench and pressed down — a grounding gesture, her palms against a surface whose vibration signature she knew and trusted, the familiar cold composite of the *Meridian*'s interior. The workbench vibrated at the ship's resting frequency. Through her left hand, the vibration was what it had always been. Through her right hand, something was different — not louder, not more intense, but *layered*. The standard vibration was there, the ship's bones humming as they always hummed. And beneath it, carried on the same structural medium but occupying a different register, a slow pulse. 0.0037 Hz. The Anomaly's breath, transmitted through the colony's infrastructure, received by two fingertip nodes that had been rebuilt to hear it.

She lifted her hands and left the maintenance bay.

## The Corridor

The residential ring was a part of the colony Sable had always transited rather than inhabited. Her quarters were here, and the mess hall, and the communal hygiene facilities — the domestic infrastructure of a working station — but she moved through these spaces the way water moves through a pipe: efficiently, directionally, without lingering in the architecture. Three seasons of this route had taught her its vibration signature so thoroughly that she no longer read it consciously; it was baseline, the thing she measured other signatures against.

Today she was not transiting. She was walking slowly, and she was reading.

Maret's summons had arrived thirty minutes after she left the *Meridian*: a system notification on her personal terminal, Archive administrative format, location and time. She had ninety minutes before the meeting. She had chosen a route through the residential section of the inner ring rather than the more direct corridor that connected the dive bay to the administrative level — a choice she registered as unusual even as she made it. She was not in a hurry. The recalibration results needed time to settle, and the settling would happen better in motion than at a desk.

The corridor was wide enough for three people abreast — residential standard, designed for the small daily commerce of bodies moving between quarters, work shifts, and communal spaces. The overhead lights were at standard residential output: warmer than the engineering sections, cooler than the administrative level, calibrated to approximate a late-afternoon register that the colony's designers had apparently believed was optimal for human comfort. Through the floor, Sable felt the composite signatures of habitation — the slow thermal gradients of occupied quarters on either side, the periodic vibration of doors opening and closing, the distant percussion of children's feet in a play area three sections forward.

She passed a door and slowed.

The door was standard residential issue — she had passed four hundred doors of identical manufacture in three seasons. But this one carried an addition. Above the frame, mounted in a bracket that was not standard issue, was a small object. Approximately eight centimeters long, irregular in shape, a muted amber color that caught the corridor light and held it. An acoustic fossil. Not an Archive specimen — Archive specimens were housed in climate-controlled display cases with classification tags, UV-filtered lighting, and vibration-dampening mounts. This one was mounted in a bracket made of bent composite stock, the kind of material available in the colony's general fabrication workshop. Someone had made the bracket by hand. The bends were not perfectly uniform — she could see the slight variation in angle that distinguished manual work from machine-cut precision. And the bracket was not surface-mounted. It was drilled into the structural composite of the corridor wall, anchored to the load-bearing layer beneath the decorative laminate. Someone had made a permanent decision about this object. It was not decoration that could be removed at the end of a lease cycle. It was *installed*. It was infrastructure.

She read the fossil from where she stood, approximately five meters away, through the floor vibration that connected her feet to the wall that connected the wall to the bracket that connected the bracket to the fossil. The signal was faint — attenuated by distance and material interfaces — but her transducers were designed for exactly this kind of indirect reading, the same principle that allowed her to assess a fossil's density through the *Meridian*'s hull during a dive. The fossil's thermal signature was room temperature, ambient, unremarkable. Its density was moderate — a Class 2 or 3, nothing rare, nothing valuable enough for the Archive to catalog with priority. A common geological compression event, preserved in crystal, worth perhaps a month's supplementary income on the open market.

But the surface. The surface told a different story. The fossil's tactile profile, transmitted through four material interfaces to her feet, carried the unmistakable signature of *handling*. Archive specimens developed a specific kind of surface wear — uniform, distributed, the product of gloved hands lifting them in and out of display cradles during inventory checks. This wear was different. It was concentrated. The face of the fossil — the broad, slightly convex surface that would rest naturally against a palm — had been touched so many times that the crystal's micro-structure had been polished by skin contact alone. Not gloved contact. Bare fingers. The same fingers, over and over, pressing the same surface in the same gesture, leaving a wear pattern as distinctive as a fingerprint. Someone held this fossil. Regularly. Repeatedly. With the sustained, specific attention of a practice rather than a habit.

Sable did not stop walking. But her pace shifted — a deceleration of perhaps fifteen percent, imperceptible to anyone without a trained eye for gait analysis, sufficient for her transducers to complete the reading. The fossil sat in its hand-made bracket above the maintenance technician's door, and it was not an object on display. It was an object in use. An object someone touched the way Sable touched the *Meridian*'s drive housing during pre-dive checks — not to assess, not to diagnose, but to *confirm*. To verify that the thing was still there, still real, still carrying whatever quality the touching was meant to contact.

She passed the door. The corridor continued. She did not look back, but the reading stayed with her — the first evidence she had encountered in three seasons on Coda that the colony's relationship to acoustic matter extended beyond the institutional framework of the Archive. The Archive classified fossils. The Archive valued fossils. The Archive stored, catalogued, and distributed fossils according to a taxonomy that began with geological origin and ended with market price. But the person behind that door had drilled a bracket into a load-bearing wall and placed a fossil in it and touched it until the crystal wore smooth, and the gesture was not institutional. It was not commercial. It was something Sable did not have a word for in the vocabulary she had built over three seasons of reading Coda's vibrational grammar, because the vocabulary she had built was an institutional vocabulary, and the thing above that door was not an institution's object.

It was a devotion.

Every data field below the header was blank.

Not redacted. Blank. Redaction left a trace — a formatting artifact, a field-length indicator that told you how much data had been removed, the bureaucratic scar of information that had been present and then excised. These fields had no trace. They were the width and depth of fields that had never been populated. As if the station had been registered — the designation assigned, the location filed, the administrative authority confirmed — and then nothing had ever happened there. No personnel records. No operational logs. No atmospheric data. No fossil inventory. No maintenance schedules, no supply requisitions, no communication archives. The station existed as a bureaucratic fact and a physical absence. A line in the directory pointing at a place that the directory's own records indicated had never been occupied.

Sable sat across from Maret. Thresh stood to her left, translating — his hands moving in the steady, precise rhythm she had learned to read as professional mode, the translation register he used when the content was complex and the margin for interpretive error was zero. Maret spoke in measured sentences, each one complete before the next began, and Thresh rendered them with a fidelity that left no space between the speaker's meaning and the receiver's understanding.

She studied the directory entry. The blank fields had a quality she recognized from her fossil work — the quality of something that should contain information and does not, the way a fossil with an empty acoustic signature reads as *wrong* rather than *silent*. Silence was the absence of signal. This was the absence of *record*. The distinction mattered. A station that had never been occupied would not have been registered. The Archive did not assign designations to unbuilt facilities. The registration date — 2108 — was sixty-three years ago. Sixty-three years of a station existing in name and not in any other dimension.

*When was the last populated entry?* she signed. *Before the fields went blank. What is the most recent dated record associated with 4-22-6?*

Thresh translated. Maret's expression — the controlled stillness she had catalogued, the administrative mask that served as both protection and communication — shifted by the increment she had learned to measure. Not surprise. Recognition. The recognition of someone whose model of the person across the desk had just been confirmed.

He spoke. Thresh:

*He says: There is no last entry. The fields were not emptied sequentially. They were emptied simultaneously. Sable looked at the number. A single clock cycle. Not a gradual degradation, not a cascading failure, not a human administrator working through fields one at a time with a deletion tool. Everything, at once, in the smallest unit of time the Archive's systems could measure. A station's entire operational history — the accumulated record of every person who had worked there, every fossil they had catalogued, every atmospheric reading they had taken, every meal they had eaten and every shift they had logged — compressed into a single moment of absence.

*What was on 4-22-6?* she signed. *Before the event. What kind of station?*

Maret paused. Not the controlled pause of someone choosing words — the shorter pause of someone deciding how much to say. She read the decision in the way his weight shifted: a transfer from his right foot to his left, approximately two kilograms of redistribution, the body's way of processing a choice the mind was still making.

He spoke. Thresh:

*He says: A deep-atmospheric survey station. Same function as Coda. Smaller — approximately six hundred registered personnel, based on the designation's registration-class code. The classification region places it above a gas giant in the same stellar neighborhood as Vespers. Different system, similar planetary characteristics. The survey mandate would have been acoustic paleontology — the same work Coda does. Fossil harvesting, classification, Archive contribution.*

A station like Coda. Smaller. Doing the same work. Sixty-three years ago.

*And the Anomaly?* Sable signed. *Was there a signal at 4-22-6?*

Maret did not answer immediately. He looked at her — not with the administrative assessment she had grown accustomed to, but with something she had not seen in him before. A weariness that lived beneath the stillness the way the Anomaly's carrier frequency lived beneath the colony's ambient vibration: always present, rarely surfaced, detectable only if you knew where to look.

He spoke. Thresh:

*He says: He does not know. The records that would tell him are in the blank fields. What he knows is what survived: the designation, the registration metadata, the single-clock-cycle event, and the calibration weight. He has spent eleven years reconstructing what he can from indirect evidence — cargo manifests, transit logs, personnel transfer records from other stations that referenced 4-22-6 as a point of origin. The indirect evidence is consistent with a station that existed, operated normally, and then stopped existing in a way that retroactively removed its operational history from every system that had recorded it. Not deleted. Not redacted. Removed from causality. As if the station had been unbuilt, and the universe had rearranged itself to accommodate the unbuilding.*

Sable sat with this. The word *unbuilding* arrived through Thresh's translation with the particular weight of a concept that had no comfortable home in the language — a word assembled from components that fit together grammatically but resisted each other semantically. You could unbuild a structure. You could not unbuild a *history*. History was not architecture. It did not have load-bearing walls that could be removed.

Except that it did. Station 4-22-6 proved that it did.

*Has anyone ever attempted to classify the Anomaly directly?* she signed. The question was precise — she was asking about the thing in the fossil, the voice, the signal that had overloaded her transducers and begun rewriting the colony's reality. Not the Resonance's effects. The source.

Thresh translated. Maret answered, and the answer arrived through Thresh's hands with the quality of something that had been waiting a long time to be said:

*He says: We classified it. That is what the entry we no longer have was.*

The sentence sat in the air between them. Sable read it — not through Thresh's hands, which had already moved to resting position, but through the silence that followed, the quality of a room in which something has been said that changes the architecture of the conversation. Maret had classified the Anomaly. Not Maret personally — the institution. The Archive. Station 4-22-6 had captured or encountered the same signal, and they had done what the Archive's systems were designed to do: they had classified it. They had given it a designation, a frequency profile, a density rating, a position in the taxonomy. They had processed it through the same institutional machinery that processed every fossil, every atmospheric event, every acoustic artifact that the deep-survey stations pulled from the crushing depths of gas giants across the sector.

And then the classification, and the station, and the people, and the history had been removed from the causal record in a single clock cycle.

The Anomaly had been classified. The classification had not survived. The act of classifying it — of processing it through the Archive's systems, of placing it within the institutional framework that gave acoustic matter its meaning and its value — had triggered the event. Or had been part of the event. Or had been the event itself, experienced from the inside.

Sable stood. Maret watched her stand. His hands were on the desk surface — not pressing down with the excess force she had observed in their previous meeting, but resting, flat, the posture of someone who had delivered the thing he had been carrying and was now lighter by exactly the weight of it.

He spoke. One word. Thresh:

*Yes.*

She nodded. Not agreement — acknowledgment. She had received the data. She would process it. She turned toward the door.

Maret spoke again. Thresh's hands moved:

*He says: Sable. The protocol I am executing is not the protocol from 4-22-6. It is the protocol I built after 4-22-6, based on what I believe happened there. They classified it and died. My protocol begins where theirs ended: with the assumption that the signal cannot be classified, contained, or institutionally processed without consequence. Everything I have done — the dive rotation stand-down, the informational quarantine, the sectoring — is designed to prevent this station from doing what 4-22-6 did. From processing the signal. From giving it a place in the system. Because every place it is given becomes a place it can erase.*

She turned back. She looked at him. He was still standing behind his desk, his hands still resting on its surface, and she saw in his posture the architecture of a man who had been building a wall for eleven years against something he had never fully seen — building it from indirect evidence and institutional resources and the single data point of a calibration weight three milligrams wrong. He was not the protocol. He was the person who had built the protocol because no one else knew there was something to build it against.

She left the office.

## The Medical Unit

The corridor from the administrative level to the residential ring's medical section was seventy-eight meters. She covered it in sixty-two seconds, and the transit contained nothing that required documentation — the same composite walls, the same lighting, the same vibration signature she had walked through ten thousand times. She did not read it. She did not need to. The colony's known architecture was not the thing she was paying attention to anymore.

Thresh walked beside her. He had not signed anything since they left Maret's office. His silence was not the operational silence of their dive work — the purposeful quiet of two people managing a complex task in tandem. It was the silence of someone processing information at a depth that did not yet have language. She recognized it because she was doing the same thing.

The medical unit occupied a section of the inner residential ring, a space Sable had entered perhaps twice in three seasons — once for her annual transducer certification, once when a supply-line pressure fitting had cut her forearm during a maintenance shift. It was a small facility: four examination rooms, a recovery bay, an equipment closet, and an administrative desk staffed by a single Archive medical technician during day shifts. The vibration signature was different from the residential corridors — quieter, more controlled, the walls treated with a dampening layer that attenuated the colony's background mechanical noise. A space designed for bodies that needed careful reading.

Senne was in the recovery bay. Through the observation window — a pressure-rated composite panel, slightly thicker than standard, designed to maintain visual access while isolating the bay's atmospheric controls — Sable could see her. She was sitting upright on the examination bed, her legs over the side, her hands in her lap. Her posture was correct — spine aligned, shoulders level, the posture of someone who had been told to sit and was complying with the instruction. But the quality of the posture was different from the Senne that Sable had met in the quarters, the Senne who had described the room with the precision of an analysis technician cataloguing real data. That Senne had been *still* with the learned stillness of a person who had chosen to be still. This Senne was still because the alternative to stillness had become uncertain.

Thresh entered the bay. Sable watched through the window.

He walked to his sister and signed: *Senne. It's Thresh.*

Senne looked up. The recognition arrived — Sable could see it in the reorganization of Senne's facial muscles, the specific softening around the eyes that meant *I know you* — but it arrived with a delay. Not the two-second delay of their previous visit. Shorter now — perhaps one second, perhaps less. But the delay was *there*, a gap between perception and identification that should not have existed between siblings, and Sable noted it the way she noted everything: as data, as measurement, as a point on a curve she was beginning to understand.

Senne's hands came up and signed Thresh's name. The sign was correct. The gesture was fluid. And Senne's face, as she made it, carried the expression of someone who had just passed a test she had not been aware she was taking.

Thresh sat beside her on the examination bed. They did not talk about her condition. They did not talk about the room, or the Resonance, or the tremor that Sable could see in Senne's hands even through the observation window — finer now than it had been, faster, as if the frequency had increased while the amplitude had decreased, the body learning to accommodate the signal by distributing it across a broader range of motion. They talked about an instrument.

Thresh had brought a calibration probe — not the one he had disassembled in the mess hall, a different one, a smaller model used for verifying the frequency response of the medical unit's diagnostic equipment. He held it out to Senne. She took it. Her hands moved over it with the automatic competence of someone who had handled similar instruments for years — turning it, checking the contact points, testing the tension of the adjustment mechanism. Thresh pointed to the frequency dial. Senne adjusted it. They discussed — through signs that Sable could read through the window, the conversation conducted in the cabin language they shared — whether the dial's detent mechanism was wearing unevenly, whether the third position clicked with less resistance than it should, whether the probe needed to be sent to maintenance or whether the wear was within acceptable tolerance.

Sable watched Thresh's hands. He was holding the conversation with the same steady precision he applied to tether operations during a dive: every sign deliberate, every question specific, every response tracked. But his left hand — the one not signing — was resting on his knee, and his index finger was tapping against his kneecap at a rhythm Sable recognized. It was the rhythm he used when checking the *Meridian*'s tether tension: a four-beat pattern, tap-tap-tap-hold, that he repeated unconsciously whenever he was monitoring a system under stress. He was not monitoring a tether. He was monitoring his composure. The calibration probe conversation was not a conversation about a calibration probe. It was a structure — a scaffolding of familiar, manageable content erected around the thing that was not manageable, the thing sitting on the examination bed in the body of his sister signing his name one second too late.

Sable understood the scaffolding because she built the same kind. The diagnostic cradle that morning. The maintenance log entry with its careful, defensible language. The word *frequency drift, non-standard* where the word *attunement* should have been. They were, both of them, people who managed the unmanageable by giving it the shape of work — by holding instruments they did not need to hold, by calibrating equipment that did not need to be calibrated, by turning the unbearable into the procedural because the procedural was a room they knew how to be in.

Thresh looked up. Through the window, his eyes found hers. The expression on his face was the one she did not have a sign for — the rearrangement she had first seen in the refinement lab, the one that meant something was happening inside him that he could not translate into the vocabulary they shared. But it was quieter now. Controlled. Held in place by the scaffolding of the calibration probe and the conversation about detent mechanisms and the four-beat tap against his knee. He was managing. He was managing the way a structure manages load: by distributing it, by transferring it through members designed to bear weight, by not concentrating it in any single point. But structures had limits. She could see his.

She entered the bay. Senne looked at her and the recognition was faster this time — not the delayed retrieval of a previous visit but something closer to normal, the eyes finding and identifying a known face within the expected window. Progress, or fluctuation. Sable did not know which, and the not-knowing was itself a data point: the Resonance's effects on biological memory were not linear. They oscillated. Good moments and bad moments, clarity and confusion, the signal fading in and out like a broadcast from a source whose power output was not constant.

*How are you?* Sable signed to Senne.

*I am here,* Senne signed back. The answer was precise in a way that Sable recognized as intentional — not a social pleasantry but a location report. *I am here* meant *I know where I am, I know when I am, I know who you are.* It was the answer of someone who had learned that these things could not be taken for granted and was confirming them the way a pilot confirms altitude: not because the information is uncertain but because the confirmation itself is the point.

Sable nodded. She placed her hand briefly on Senne's shoulder — a gesture she did not make often, a gesture that meant something different from her usual tactile interactions because it was not diagnostic. She was not reading Senne's skin temperature or muscle tension or pulse rate. She was touching her the way the person behind the residential door touched the fossil in the hand-made bracket: to confirm that the thing was still there.

She left the bay. Thresh remained. Through the window, she saw him pick up the calibration probe and resume the conversation about the detent mechanism, and Senne's hands moved over the instrument with the same automatic competence, and the tremor was there in her fingers but the competence was there too, and the two things existed in the same hands at the same time, and Sable turned away.

The corridor outside the medical unit was quiet. Residential ring, inner section, medical dampening layer in the walls. She stood still and pressed her palms flat against the floor — a gesture that would have looked like someone who had dropped something, except that she held it, held the contact, held the reading for approximately fifteen seconds.

The colony's vibration signature through the residential ring floor was not the signature she had carried in her body for three seasons. The difference was small — she would not have detected it through boots, only through direct transducer contact with the composite surface. The ambient temperature of the colony's structural vibration, as measured through the medical section's floor, was approximately two degrees above the baseline she had maintained in her personal environmental log for three seasons. Two degrees. The margin of error on her transducer system's thermal sensitivity was 0.3 degrees. This was not noise. The colony was warmer. Not warmer in the atmospheric sense — the air temperature was unchanged. Warmer in the structural sense. The composite material that constituted the colony's skeleton was carrying a thermal load it had not been carrying before.

She stood. She walked to her quarters. She opened her encrypted local log — the one she had taken off the Archive network, the one Maret could access but could not delete because it lived on hardware she physically controlled — and entered two notations.

The first: *Residential ring structural vibration: +2.0°C above three-season baseline. Not atmospheric. Structural. Source unknown.*

The second, after a pause during which she sat at her desk and looked at the wall and thought about a calibration weight that was three milligrams wrong and a diagnostic cradle that had told her what her body already knew and a fossil above a stranger's door that had been touched until it was smooth and a sister who signed her brother's name one second late:

*The baseline may no longer be reliable.*
