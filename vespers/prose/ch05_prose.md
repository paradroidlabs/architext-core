# Chapter 5 — The Threshold

## The Summons

The note was paper.

Sable found it at 09:40, slid beneath her door with the precision of someone who understood that paper, on a station where all communication ran through networked systems, was itself a statement. The paper was Archive-stock — she recognized the weight and grain through her fingertips before she read the words. Standard notation format, the same format used for fossil classification entries and post-dive documentation. Two lines, handwritten:

*Administrative Suite 3. 10:00.*

No name. No credential. No subject line. The handwriting was small and controlled, each character formed with the deliberateness of someone accustomed to writing in systems where legibility was not optional. She had never seen Maret's handwriting. She did not need to have seen it to know whose it was.

She looked at the note for approximately thirty seconds. Then she folded it once, placed it in the pocket of her work jacket, and went to find Thresh.

He was in the mess hall. She saw him before he saw her — seated at the far end of the second table, his hands wrapped around a drink container in the particular configuration she recognized as *holding something in order to have something to hold.* The calibration tool was on the table beside his tray, disassembled into its three component sections. He had taken it apart and not put it back together. His food was untouched.

She sat across from him. His eyes came up and his hands followed, the drink container released.

*Maret wants to meet,* she signed. *In person. Ten o'clock.*

Thresh's expression did not change. This was itself information — the absence of surprise meant he had been expecting this, or something like it, since the date had appeared in the post-dive report.

*Where?*

*Administrative Suite 3.*

*I'm coming.*

It was not a question. She had not intended to go without him. She nodded.

They left the mess hall at 09:51. The colony's morning vibration was unchanged from the pattern Sable had catalogued across three seasons — atmospheric processors at standard load, freight elevator cycling on its regular schedule, the composite hum of four thousand people and their machinery conducting their Tuesday. She noted this normalcy with the specific attention of someone who understood it was about to end.

Administrative Suite 3 was on the inner ring, Level 2, in a corridor Sable had walked past approximately four hundred times without entering. The door was standard Archive administrative issue — heavier gauge than residential, a slightly different vibration signature when it opened because the hinges were rated for pressure-seal emergency closure. The vibration told her it had been opened recently and often. Maret used this office.

The room was small. A desk, a chair behind it, two chairs in front. A terminal on the desk, dark. Overhead lighting at standard institutional output. The walls were bare — no framed documents, no personal effects, no coastline photograph like Prael's office, no geode bookend, no evidence that a human being spent significant time here. The room had the quality of a space that was *used* rather than *inhabited*, the way a surgical theater is used: for specific procedures, then vacated.

One object broke the pattern.

On the desk, to the left of the dark terminal, sat a container. Cylindrical, approximately twenty centimeters tall, brushed titanium casing with a vacuum-seal indicator strip running vertically along one side. The indicator strip was green — seal intact. The container was the kind used for interstellar specimen transit: triple-walled, internally dampened, rated for the thermal and pressure extremes of long-haul cargo transport between star systems. Sable had seen containers like it in the Archive's deep-storage vaults. They were not standard equipment for an administrative office.

Maret was standing behind the desk. Not seated — standing, the way he had been standing in the intake corridor the first time she saw him: a chosen position, not a resting one. He was wearing the same administrative clothing she had observed in that first encounter, and the cuffs were the same — worn, washed too many times, the fabric thinned at the fold lines. The wear had not changed in the intervening days. It was old wear. Years of it.

He looked at her. She looked at him. Neither of them communicated anything for approximately four seconds, which was long enough for Sable to catalogue the room's vibration signature (minimal — the administrative ring was structurally isolated from the colony's heavy machinery) and Maret's physiological state as readable through visual observation (controlled breathing, shoulders set but not rigid, weight evenly distributed — a man who had prepared for this conversation).

Then Maret spoke. She watched Thresh's hands.

*He says: I read your cross-reference note.*

Sable waited.

*He says: You found it faster than I expected. I had estimated another thirty-six hours before you reached the classification index. Your methodology is not what I modeled.*

She felt something shift in the room's relational architecture — not physical, not vibrational, but structural in the way a conversation has structure. Maret was not interrogating her. He was *assessing* her, the way she assessed a fossil field: comparing what he observed against a pre-existing model, noting the deviations. He had a model of how a diver would investigate the Resonance. She had deviated from it. This interested him.

*Tell him I found it because I read the whole index,* she signed to Thresh. *Not because I searched for it. I read the system so the gap would present itself against the baseline. His model assumed a targeted search. I don't do targeted searches. I do inventories.*

Thresh translated. Maret listened. His expression — the same controlled stillness she had observed in the intake corridor, the face that did not react when she said *structured* — shifted. Not much. The muscles around his eyes changed configuration by perhaps two millimeters. It was the expression of someone recalibrating.

He spoke again. Thresh's hands:

*He says: I owe you context. Not because you have earned it — you have, but that is not why. Because the context changes what the data means, and you are making decisions based on incomplete data, and incomplete data in this situation is more dangerous than no data at all.*

## The Weight

Maret reached for the container on his desk. He turned it so the vacuum-seal indicator faced Sable. Green. He placed his thumb on the biometric release — a security measure she had only seen on containers rated for Class 7 specimens, the highest classification in the Archive's system — and the seal broke with a soft percussive release she felt through the desk surface as a single, brief pressure change.

He opened the container. Inside, nested in a suspension cradle designed to eliminate vibration and thermal transfer, was a small object. A cylinder, approximately four centimeters long and two centimeters in diameter, machined from a dull silver alloy with a matte finish. A calibration weight. Standard Archive issue — she had seen hundreds of them in the refinement labs, used to verify the mass-measurement systems that determined fossil density and compression ratios. They were among the most precisely manufactured objects in the Archive's inventory: mass-verified to fourteen decimal places, certified annually, replaced at the first sign of surface oxidation or mechanical stress. They were, by design and by function, the most *real* objects in the Archive — the baseline against which all other measurements were taken. If a calibration weight said it weighed 10.00000000000000 grams, then 10.00000000000000 grams was what ten grams *was.*

Maret placed the weight on the desk between them. Then he placed a second object beside it: a printed certificate, Archive watermark, the verification record for the weight. She read the certificate. Mass at last verification: 10.00000000000000 grams. Verification date: 2167-03-28. Verifying station: 4-22-6.

Fourteen days before the date Maret had written in her report.

Maret spoke. Thresh:

*He says: This weight was in transit between Station 4-22-6 and a calibration laboratory on Waystation Oriel when the event occurred. It was in a cargo container on a long-haul freighter. The freighter arrived at Oriel eleven days after the event. The cargo manifest listed the weight. The receiving laboratory ran standard intake verification.*

Pause. Thresh's hands reorganized. She recognized this — the translation was encountering content that resisted their sign system's vocabulary.

*He says: The weight's mass at intake was 10.00300000000000 grams.*

Sable looked at the weight on the desk.

*He says: The discrepancy is 0.003 grams. Three milligrams. The laboratory initially classified it as a manufacturing defect and flagged the weight for return. Then they checked the freighter's cargo manifest against Station 4-22-6's inventory. Station 4-22-6 had no inventory. Station 4-22-6 had no records of any kind. The station designation existed in the Archive directory. Everything the designation pointed to was empty.*

Maret paused. She watched his face. The controlled stillness was there, but beneath it — or through it, the way a frequency bleeds through a harmonic filter — she detected something she had not seen in him before. It was not fear. It was the residue of fear, long processed, worn down to a persistent low-grade hum the way the cuffs of his jacket had been worn down: by sustained, repetitive contact with something abrasive.

*He says: The weight is the only physical object that survived from Station 4-22-6. Everything else — the station, the people, the fossils, the Archive records, the atmospheric data, the colonial infrastructure — was removed from the causal record. This weight was preserved because it was not at the station when the removal occurred. It was in a vacuum-sealed container in a cargo hold in transit between two points in space, and whatever mechanism the Resonance uses to erase causality did not extend to objects in transit.*

Another pause. Maret's breathing had not changed. His posture had not changed. But his hands — she noticed this — his hands were flat on the desk surface on either side of the calibration weight, fingers slightly spread, pressing down with a pressure she could estimate from the whitening of his nail beds: approximately fifteen percent more than a relaxed hand would exert. He was holding himself to the desk.

*He says: The weight's mass should not have changed. Calibration weights are inert. They do not interact chemically with their containers. They do not lose or gain mass in vacuum. The change is not oxidation, not contamination, not thermal expansion. The change is in the mass itself. The object is three milligrams heavier than it was when it left a station that no longer exists.*

Sable placed her fingertip on the weight. The transducers registered it: cool, smooth, dense, the particular thermal signature of machined alloy at room temperature. Standard. Ordinary. The most ordinary object in the room, and in the Archive, and in the entire institutional apparatus of acoustic fossil classification — because its ordinariness was its *function*. It existed to be the thing against which extraordinariness was measured.

And it was wrong.

Three milligrams. A quantity so small it could not be felt, could not be seen, would not register on any instrument less precise than a laboratory mass comparator. Three milligrams that meant the weight had been *changed* by the same event that had erased the station from causality — changed subtly, changed in the one dimension that mattered most for its purpose, changed in a way that made it *unreliable as a standard of measurement.*

If the calibration weight was wrong, then every measurement taken using it as a reference was wrong. Every fossil density reading, every compression ratio, every mass verification at Station 4-22-6 for the duration of the weight's service had been calibrated against a baseline that was no longer a baseline. And if the Resonance could alter a calibration weight — the simplest, most inert, most definitionally *real* object in the Archive — then it could alter anything. The question was not what the Resonance had changed. The question was what it had *not* changed, and whether anyone could verify the difference.

She removed her fingertip from the weight.

*How long have you had this?* she signed.

Thresh translated. Maret answered.

*He says: Eleven years. He was assigned to the investigation of the 4-22-6 event as a junior archivist. The investigation concluded that the event was a localized anomaly — a classification failure cascading through a single station's systems. The weight discrepancy was noted in the final report as an unresolved measurement artifact. He is the only person who read the report who understood what the discrepancy meant.*

*What does it mean to him?* she signed.

Maret spoke. Thresh's hands moved slowly, carefully, each sign placed with the deliberateness of someone building a structure that needed to bear weight.

*He says: It means the Resonance does not begin when you detect it. It means the instruments you use to detect it have already been compromised by the time you think to look. The protocol he inherited was designed for a threat that announces itself. The Resonance does not announce itself. It edits the system you would use to find it, and then it waits.*

## Senne

Thresh took her to Senne at 10:48.

He did not sign the request. He stood in the corridor outside Administrative Suite 3 after Maret's door had closed behind them, and he looked at Sable, and his hands came up and stopped and came down again — the gesture of someone who had something to say and had decided that saying it was less efficient than simply going. He turned and walked. She followed.

Senne's quarters were in the residential ring, Section 7, a corridor Sable knew from the survey — she had walked this section that morning cataloguing the stillness in the mess hall, the man touching his own forearm, the child drawing the room. The corridor's vibration signature was the same. The lights were at standard residential output. Everything was the same.

Thresh knocked. The door opened. Senne was inside.

She was seated at her workstation. The display was active — Sable could see the backlight reflected in Senne's eyes — but her hands were in her lap, not on the interface. She was looking at the screen the way people look at objects they have forgotten the purpose of: with a recognition that does not reach comprehension. The stillness Sable had noted in their first meeting — the deliberate, learned stillness of someone who had trained herself to occupy rooms without dominating them — was different now. It was not deliberate. It was the stillness of something that had been *stilled.*

Thresh signed: *Senne.*

Senne turned. Her eyes found him. She looked at his face for approximately two seconds — a duration Sable measured with the involuntary precision of someone who understood that two seconds was wrong. Two seconds was the time it took to recognize a stranger. Senne had known Thresh for the entire duration of Thresh's life.

Then Senne signed his name. The sign was correct — the specific hand-shape and motion they had established in their shared cabin language — but it arrived late, and it arrived with a quality Sable recognized from her own experience with the phantom: the quality of something retrieved from a place that was not quite the right place. Senne had found his name. She had found it the way you find an object that has been moved from its usual shelf — you know it exists, you know what it looks like, but the location is wrong, and the wrongness makes the recognition feel borrowed rather than owned.

*How are you?* Thresh signed.

Senne looked at Sable. Not at Thresh. At Sable. Her gaze was direct and specific, with the quality of someone who was seeing something she had been told about but had not previously encountered.

She spoke. Thresh translated, and Sable watched the translation arrive with the particular delay that meant the content was difficult to map:

*She says: Are you the one who listens?*

Sable's transducers flared. A brief, sharp pulse through the spinal array — the phantom's signature, unmistakable, arriving and departing in under a second. She kept her expression unchanged.

*Tell her I don't listen,* Sable signed. *Tell her I feel.*

Thresh translated. Senne considered this. Then she nodded — slowly, with the gravity of someone confirming something important — and she raised her hands and began to describe the room.

The description was different from the one Thresh had relayed that morning. Not contradictory — expanded. The high ceiling was there, the amber walls with their grown-material grain, the ambient light, the concave console. But now there were new details, and the new details arrived with the clinical specificity of an analysis technician whose training had survived whatever the Resonance was doing to the rest of her:

The console surface was warm. Not room temperature — elevated, by approximately three degrees above ambient. The warmth was uniform across the surface, which meant it was generated from within the material, not applied externally.

There were marks on the console. Not writing, not symbols, not interface elements. Impressions. As if someone had placed their fingers against the surface and the material had yielded slightly, retaining the shape the way clay retains a fingerprint. But the material was not clay — it was the same amber-grained substance as the walls, which Senne had described as harder than stone. Whatever had made the impressions had pressed with enough force, or enough *specificity*, to deform a surface that should not deform.

Seven impressions. Arranged in a vertical line along the center of the console, spaced at irregular intervals that Senne described as *approximately two centimeters, then three, then two, then three, then two, then three.*

Sable's hand went to her own spine. Not consciously — the gesture preceded thought, the body reaching for the thing the mind had not yet named. Seven nodes. Seven transducers, grafted along her spinal column in a vertical line, spaced at intervals determined by the curvature of her vertebrae: approximately two centimeters between the first and second, three between the second and third, two, three, two, three.

The impressions on the console matched the spacing of her spinal transducers.

She lowered her hand. She looked at Senne. Senne was watching her with an expression that contained something Sable did not want to name — not recognition, not comprehension, but *acknowledgment*. The look of someone who had seen a pattern they could not interpret but could verify was present.

Sable reached forward and took Senne's hand. Not to communicate — to diagnose. She pressed her fingertip transducers against Senne's wrist, over the radial pulse point, and held.

Senne's skin temperature was 38.4 degrees — elevated by approximately 1.2 degrees above the human baseline Sable used for wrist-pulse assessment. Her pulse was present but irregular: a normal sinus rhythm interrupted every eight to twelve beats by a premature contraction that Sable felt as a brief thickening of the pulse followed by a compensatory pause. The irregularity was not dangerous — not yet — but it was not Senne's baseline. Senne was twenty-four. Healthy analysis technicians of twenty-four did not have premature atrial contractions at a frequency of one per eight beats.

Senne's hands were trembling. Sable could feel it through the contact: a rhythmic oscillation at approximately four hertz, visible in the fingers, transmitted through the wrist. Not the fine interior vibration Sable had experienced during the phantom revelation — this was coarser, more mechanical, the body's thermoregulatory or neurological systems expressing something they had not been designed to express. The tremor was continuous. Senne did not appear to be aware of it.

Sable released Senne's wrist. She looked at Thresh. He was standing in the doorway, his hands at his sides, the calibration tool nowhere in evidence — he had left it disassembled on the mess hall table, the first time in her memory he had left it anywhere. His face was doing the thing it had done in the refinement lab when she had told him about the pod and his sister: the specific rearrangement that she did not have a sign for. But it was worse now. It was worse because Senne was present and functional and speaking and wrong, and the wrongness was not a dramatic disintegration but a gradual editorial process — a sentence being rewritten while you read it, the meaning shifting beneath the familiar words.

*Her pulse is irregular,* Sable signed to Thresh. *Premature contractions. Temperature elevated. Tremor at four hertz. These are autonomic symptoms — the nervous system, not the conscious mind. The Resonance is editing below the level of identity now. It started with her memories. It's in her body.*

Thresh looked at his sister. Senne was looking at the workstation screen again, at the display she had forgotten how to use, and her hands were trembling in her lap with the metronomic regularity of a signal being received.

## The Wall

They stepped into the corridor and the corridor was wrong.

Not visually. The lights were at standard residential output. The walls were the same composite laminate she had walked past for three seasons. The floor was the same. The ceiling was the same. But the vibration signature — the thing Sable read the way hearing people read ambient sound, the continuous background data that told her the state of every mechanical system within structural transmission range — had changed.

The atmospheric processors were cycling at a frequency she had not felt before. Faster, by approximately seven percent, with a harmonic she did not recognize — a secondary oscillation laid over the primary cycle, as if the processors had been given an additional task. The freight elevator was silent. Not cycling, not loading, not moving. Still. The elevator had run on a continuous schedule for the entirety of her three seasons on Coda; she had used its rhythm as a timing reference the way a hearing person might use a clock's tick. Its absence was not silence — there was no silence for Sable, only the redistribution of vibrational energy into other channels — but it was a *gap* in the colony's operational heartbeat.

And the bulkheads.

She felt them before she saw them: a change in the corridor's acoustic profile, the way vibration traveled through the floor and walls altered by the presence of a heavy steel barrier that had not been there when they entered Senne's quarters forty minutes earlier. She walked to the section boundary, approximately thirty meters from Senne's door, and there it was. A pressure-rated bulkhead seal, descended from the ceiling recess where it was stored for emergency deployment, filling the corridor from wall to wall and floor to ceiling. The seal was complete — no gap at the base, no gap at the sides. The locking mechanism was engaged. She placed her palm against its surface: cold steel, thick, the vibration from the far side of the seal attenuated by approximately sixty percent. Whatever was happening on the other side of this bulkhead was happening in an acoustically isolated space.

She turned. Thresh was behind her, looking at the bulkhead with an expression she read as *confirmation* — he had been expecting this too. He had known what Maret's meeting meant. Maret had been giving them information and then doing the thing the information was about.

*The protocol,* Sable signed.

Thresh nodded.

She placed her hands against the corridor wall and listened. The colony's vibration signature was a map she had spent three seasons learning to read, and the map was being redrawn. The changes were systematic — she could feel bulkhead seals at regular intervals along the residential ring, each one a new wall in a building that had not had walls in those places that morning. The communication relays — she could feel them too, a high-frequency carrier signal that ran through the colony's structural framework, the data network's physical substrate — were running a pattern she had never encountered. Intermittent. Gated. The relays were cycling on and off in a sequence, which meant they were being *regulated* — allowing data to pass in one direction, at specified intervals, through specified sections.

Maret was sectoring the colony.

Not for medical quarantine — medical quarantine would isolate the infected, not the informed. This was *informational* quarantine. He was dividing Coda into zones and cutting communication between them. The sections where Stage 3 symptoms had been reported — she could infer which ones from the bulkhead pattern, mapping the sealed boundaries against the locations she and Thresh had surveyed that morning — were being isolated from one another. People who had experienced the room memory were being prevented from comparing notes. The shared experience was being prevented from becoming *confirmed* shared experience.

Because Maret's protocol treated the Resonance as a social contagion. Something that spread through information — through the exchange of descriptions, the comparison of symptoms, the collective realization that the room was not a personal hallucination but a shared phenomenon. His protocol said: if you prevent the information from propagating, you prevent the Resonance from propagating.

And his protocol was wrong.

Sable knew it was wrong because of the child. The child had drawn the room before breakfast, before any adult on Coda had discussed the room, before the survey, before Senne's report to Thresh. The child had experienced the room independently, spontaneously, through a mechanism that did not require social transmission. The Resonance was not spreading through conversation. It was spreading through a channel the previous protocol had not accounted for — and now Maret was executing that protocol anyway, because it was the only protocol that existed, because the alternative to a wrong protocol was no protocol at all, and Tomás Maret was a man who had carried a compromised calibration weight for eleven years rather than exist in a world without a standard of measurement.

But Sable saw something else in the protocol — something Maret could not see because he was inside it, because he had been inside it for eleven years, because you cannot read the system you are part of the way you read a system from outside.

The protocol's mechanism was *identical to the Resonance's mechanism.*

Isolation. Severance. Erasure. The Resonance was erasing Station 4-22-6 from the causal record — removing its data, cutting its connections, isolating its designation in a directory that pointed at nothing. Maret's protocol was erasing the colony's *awareness* of the Resonance — removing the shared memory from communal knowledge, cutting communication between sections, isolating the affected individuals in quarters where their experience would point at nothing because no one else would be there to confirm it.

He was fighting the Resonance by performing a bureaucratic version of its symptoms. He was applying containment by replicating the disease.

She turned from the bulkhead. Thresh was watching her, and she saw in his face the thing she had felt in her own body during the phantom revelation: the moment when a pattern resolves and the resolution is worse than the uncertainty.

*Can you get us through?* he signed.

*Yes,* she signed. *I've been reading this colony's infrastructure for three seasons. I know the override sequences for the emergency systems. The bulkheads are pressure-rated — they're designed to be opened from either side in case of atmospheric breach. Maret sealed them from the central administrative system, but the local overrides are mechanical.*

She went to the bulkhead's edge, where the seal met the corridor wall, and found the manual release housing — a recessed panel, standard on all pressure-rated emergency systems, designed to be operable even if the colony's electrical systems failed. She turned the release lever. The mechanism resisted, then yielded. The bulkhead retracted approximately eighty centimeters — enough to pass through, not enough to fully open. The vibration from the far side flooded through the gap: the atmospheric processors, still cycling at their altered frequency, and beneath that, something she had not felt before. A low, persistent warmth in the floor, radiating upward through the composite, as if the structural material itself were running a temperature it should not have.

They moved through. She sealed the bulkhead behind them.

Two sections further — past another bulkhead, another manual override, another gap to slip through — the warmth in the floor was stronger. Not uniform. Concentrated. She felt it through her boots in a pattern that was not random: a gradient, intensifying as she moved along the corridor, peaking at a specific point.

She stopped.

She crouched and placed her palms flat against the corridor floor. The composite was warm — approximately four degrees above the ambient temperature she had catalogued as the residential ring's baseline. She moved her hands slowly, mapping the thermal gradient. It was roughly circular, centered on a point approximately one meter ahead and slightly to the left of the corridor's centerline. The diameter of the warm zone was approximately two meters.

She stood and placed her right hand against the corridor wall.

The surface was composite — metal-polymer laminate, smooth, cool, institutional. She had touched this material ten thousand times in three seasons. She knew its tactile signature the way she knew the *Meridian's* drive frequency: not as data she had memorized but as data her body had *become*. The composite had a specific grain — microscopic, barely perceptible even through the transducers, the fingerprint of the manufacturing process that produced it in sheets and bonded it to the station's structural ribs.

The grain under her palm was wrong.

Not the composite's grain. A different grain. Long parallel lines running vertically, spaced at intervals she estimated as approximately one millimeter — too regular for organic material, too irregular for machine-cut. The texture of something that had been *grown*. Something with a molecular structure that organized itself along a preferred axis, the way wood grain follows the direction of growth, the way crystal lattices align along their primary formation vector.

She moved her hand. The grain extended approximately forty centimeters along the corridor wall, between two structural ribs. Above and below the affected area, the composite's normal grain resumed. The affected section was contained — a patch, a sample, a *protrusion* of something that should not have existed in this material, in this corridor, on this station.

She pressed harder. The transducers reported: surface hardness consistent with the amber material Senne had described. Temperature slightly elevated — perhaps two degrees above the surrounding composite. The parallel grain was continuous, uninterrupted, as if it had always been part of the wall, as if the wall had always been made of this.

*Thresh,* she signed. *Put your hand here.*

He placed his palm where hers had been. He held it for approximately five seconds. Then he moved it across the surface — slowly, the way she had taught him to check for thermal anomalies on the *Meridian's* hull.

*I feel the wall,* he signed. *Composite. Normal.*

He could not feel it. His hands — unaugmented, unreceivered, registering the world at the resolution of baseline human touch — felt only the corridor wall they had always known. The grain was there. The texture was there. The room was pressing through the station's physical structure, expressing itself in the material of the corridor the way it had expressed itself in the colony's memories, in Senne's calibration recordings, in the photograph on Sable's shelf. But it was arriving through touch. Through the specific, transducer-mediated tactile channel that had always been the Resonance's pathway into Sable's perception.

The room was leaking into the world. And it was arriving through *her.*

She stood in the corridor with her palm against the wall where the room had begun to rewrite the station's physical reality, and she thought: *this is what happened to 4-22-6.* Not all at once. Not in a catastrophic rupture. Gradually. Incrementally. A patch of wrong grain here. A temperature anomaly there. The room arriving in pieces, inserting itself into the existing architecture the way a revised document retains the formatting of the original — the margins the same, the font the same, only the *words* different, and if you were not reading carefully you might not notice that the meaning had changed entirely.

The Resonance was not destroying Coda. It was *revising* it. Editing the colony's physical reality the way it had edited Senne's memories, the way it had edited the photograph on the shelf, the way it had edited Station 4-22-6's classification records. The process was not violent. It was *thorough*. It was the work of something that had all the time in the world — all the time in *two* worlds — and was applying that time with a patience that dwarfed human scales of urgency.

She removed her hand from the wall.

*We need to go back to Maret,* she signed.

Thresh looked at her. His hands came up, paused, came down. Then:

*Why?*

*Because his protocol is wrong and I can prove it and if he keeps running it he is going to do the Resonance's work for it and four thousand people are going to die the way Station 4-22-6 died — not in an explosion, not in a decompression, but in an edit. A revision. They'll be here and then they'll be a gap in an index and a calibration weight that weighs three milligrams too much and nothing else.*

Thresh looked at the wall where her hand had been. He could not feel what she had felt. But he had watched her feel it, and he had spent three seasons learning to trust what her transducers told her even when his own senses reported nothing. That trust — the conditional trust he had been spending faster than he earned it since the corridor outside the mess hall, since *think fast*, since the morning's survey of six shattered people and a child who drew rooms — reached a decision point, and the decision arrived not as a word or a gesture but as a physical action: he turned toward Administrative Suite 3 and began walking.

She followed.

## The Threshold

Maret was still standing behind his desk. The calibration weight was still on the desk surface, beside its verification certificate from a station that no longer existed. He looked up when they entered, and Sable saw in his face the particular expression of a man who had expected this return visit — not because he had planned it, but because he had recognized, in her, the same quality that had kept him carrying a wrong weight for eleven years. The inability to leave a problem unsolved. The compulsion to follow data to its terminus, even when the terminus was worse than the question.

Sable did not sit. She stood across the desk from him, Thresh at her left shoulder, and she signed to Thresh and Thresh translated, and Maret listened with the stillness of someone who had been waiting a very long time for a different voice to say a thing he had been unable to say to himself.

*Your protocol is wrong.*

Thresh spoke. Maret's expression did not change.

*The protocol treats the Resonance as social contagion. You are isolating sections to prevent the shared memory from being compared and confirmed. But the child had the room memory before any adult could have described it to her. She is seven. She has no network access, no communication with the affected analysis technicians, no exposure pathway that runs through information exchange. The Resonance does not spread through conversation. It spreads through a channel your protocol does not account for, and by isolating people you are not preventing transmission — you are preventing the colony from understanding what is happening to it.*

Maret spoke. Thresh:

*He says: He knows. He says: the protocol from 4-22-6 specified informational quarantine as the first containment measure. He is executing it because it is what exists. He asks: what is the alternative.*

Sable's hands:

*Your protocol does not just fail to contain the Resonance. It replicates the Resonance's mechanism. Isolation. Severance. Erasure. You are sectoring this colony the way the Resonance sectored Station 4-22-6 — cutting connections, removing shared knowledge, replacing community with gaps. The Resonance erases by disconnecting things from their context. Your protocol erases awareness by disconnecting people from each other. You are performing a bureaucratic version of causal erasure and calling it containment.*

Thresh translated. His voice — which she could not hear but could observe in the movement of his throat, the set of his jaw, the way his chest expanded for the breath that carried the longest sentences — was steady. He was not editorializing. He was not softening. He was translating with the fidelity of someone who understood that the accuracy of this translation was the most important thing he had done in his life.

Maret was very still. The particular stillness she had identified in their first encounter — the administrative stillness, the stillness of a man whose position required him to be unmoved — was different now. It was the stillness of someone who had been told a thing they already knew, by someone who had arrived at the knowledge from a direction they had not anticipated.

He spoke. One word. Thresh:

*He says: Continue.*

Sable:

*I know what I am. The Resonance has been modeling me specifically. There are seven impressions on the console in the room — the room that every affected person on this station is experiencing. Seven. Spaced at intervals that match the seven transducers on my spinal column. The Resonance did not target me because I happened to capture the anomaly. It targeted me because I was already built to receive it. The phantom I have carried since birth — the sensation I always attributed to my mother — is the room. The Resonance has been transmitting to me for my entire life. The anomaly amplified a pre-existing signal. I am not the vector because the fossil contaminated my hardware. I am the vector because my hardware was always tuned to this frequency.*

Thresh translated. Sable watched Maret's face. The two-millimeter expression shift happened again — the recalibration, the model being adjusted to accommodate new data. He had not known this. The protocol from 4-22-6 had not accounted for a receiver. The previous event had not had one, or the previous event's receiver had been erased along with the station.

*The voice in the fossil was interrupted mid-sentence,* Sable signed. *I felt it during the capture — a voice, panicked, mid-utterance, reaching. The Resonance is not a weapon. It is not an invasion. It is the grief of something unfinished. A sentence that was never completed. A communication that was interrupted at the moment of universal collapse and has been propagating ever since, through the geology of gas giants, through the deep-time record of crystalline storms, looking for someone who could receive it. The solution is not containment. Containment is what failed at 4-22-6. The solution is completion. Let the sentence finish. Let the voice say what it was trying to say.*

Thresh stopped translating. Not because the content had exceeded his sign vocabulary — because he had run out of breath. He took a breath. He finished.

Maret looked at the calibration weight on his desk. He looked at it for a long time. The weight sat there in the light of the institutional overheads, four centimeters of machined alloy, three milligrams wrong, the only surviving artifact of a station that had been revised out of reality, and Sable saw him see it for what it was: not proof, not evidence, not a measurement artifact. A *symptom.* The Resonance had touched the weight the way it had touched Senne's memory, the way it had touched the photograph, the way it had touched the wall in the corridor. It had added three milligrams. It had added a room. It had added a face. It had added a grain to a wall. All the same gesture. All the same sentence, still trying to be spoken.

Maret spoke. One sentence.

Thresh:

*He says: What do you need?*

Four words. The question was small and the space it opened was enormous. It was not agreement — his face had not shifted into anything she would read as conviction. It was not surrender — his posture retained the architecture of a man who had been executing a protocol and had not stopped executing it. It was something more difficult than either: the acknowledgment that his protocol, the protocol from 4-22-6, the only protocol that existed, had been designed for a threat that the threat had already outgrown, and that the person standing across from him — a deaf diver with seven spinal transducers and a phantom she had carried since birth — might be the only instrument capable of measuring what was actually happening.

Sable signed:

*Time. And access to the deep-dive bathysphere specifications.*

Maret looked at her. Then he nodded. The nod was small, controlled, institutional. But his hands — still flat on the desk, still pressing down with the fifteen-percent excess pressure she had noted earlier — relaxed. Not fully. Three percent, perhaps. The beginning of a release that had been held for eleven years.

Sable turned. Thresh turned with her. They walked out of Administrative Suite 3 together, into the corridor with its sealed bulkheads and its altered vibration signature and its walls that were, in at least one forty-centimeter section, no longer entirely made of the material they had been made of that morning.

The colony hummed around them. Four thousand people, sectored and silenced and afraid, living inside a structure that was being rewritten at a frequency most of them could not detect. The transducers ran warm along Sable's spine. The phantom was there — the room, the place, the unfinished sentence — present and persistent, a signal she would not suppress because suppressing it was what the old protocol had tried and the old protocol had failed.

She walked. Thresh walked beside her. For the first time in five chapters of her life, she was not walking toward something to observe it. She was not walking toward a lab, a terminal, a classification index, a survey route. She was walking toward the deep-dive bay, where the bathysphere specifications were stored, where the engineering data for the deepest-rated vessels in the colony's fleet would tell her whether it was possible to survive a descent into the metallic hydrogen core of a gas giant.

She was walking toward an act.

Behind her, in his office, Maret picked up the calibration weight and placed it back in its vacuum-sealed container and closed the lid. The weight sat in its suspension cradle, three milligrams wrong, and the indicator strip turned green, and the room was quiet, and the cuffs of his jacket were still worn, and outside the hull of the colony the gas giant Vespers turned in its ancient orbit, and in the frozen crystal lattices of its deepest storms a voice that was older than humanity continued to grieve in a language the universe had never been built to understand but which, for the first time in fifty-nine years, someone was preparing to answer.
